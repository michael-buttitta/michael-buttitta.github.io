01 First principles

What does it mean to be intelligent?

Before asking whether a machine can be intelligent, we have to decide what intelligence is. Researchers usually break it into capabilities: learning from experience, reasoning from evidence, planning ahead, solving problems, perceiving the world, and adapting when it changes. Artificial intelligence is the engineering project of reproducing selected aspects of that list — not the project of building a human mind.

That word selected carries a lot of weight. A pocket calculator out-computes every human alive, yet nobody calls it intelligent, because raw computation is not the same thing as intelligence. A chess engine plans brilliantly inside 64 squares and is helpless outside them. Pick a system below and see which capabilities it genuinely has — the pattern that emerges is the single most important idea on this page: today’s AI is narrow, superhuman at specific tasks, rather than general, capable across all of them the way people are.

    Judgments are simplified on purpose — the point is the shape of each profile, not a precise score. Note that “creativity” gets a qualified check for generative systems: they recombine patterns from training data in genuinely novel ways, which may or may not meet your definition of the word. Consciousness is listed to make a point: no AI system has it, none is close, and none of the capabilities above require it.

    02 Seven decades

    A brief history of AI

    AI is not new. The field was named in 1956, boomed twice, collapsed twice, and has been compounding since the early 2010s. The curve below tracks the field’s momentum — funding, activity, and expectations — through both AI winters. Click any milestone to place it on the curve and read what actually happened.

    A stylized momentum curve — illustrative, not a plot of any single dataset. The shaded bands are the two AI winters.

    1. Alan Turing’s paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence opens with “Can machines think?” and immediately replaces it with a testable alternative: can a machine hold a conversation indistinguishable from a person’s? The Turing Test reframed a philosophical argument as an engineering target — and the field has been arguing about whether it’s the right target ever since.

    2. A summer workshop at Dartmouth College — organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and Nathaniel Rochester — coins the term artificial intelligence and declares the founding bet: “every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.” The attendees expected major progress in a generation. They were off by several.

    3. Two rival traditions are born almost together. Symbolic AI encodes knowledge as logic and rules — programs like Logic Theorist prove mathematical theorems. Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron (1958) instead learns from examples, an ancestor of every neural network. ELIZA (1966) fools people into confiding in a script that mostly rephrases their words — the first demonstration that appearing intelligent is easier than being intelligent.

    4. Promises collide with reality: machine translation flops, perceptrons are proven unable to solve simple problems (Minsky and Papert’s Perceptrons, 1969), and the 1973 Lighthill Report tells the British government the field has failed its objectives. Funding evaporates. The lesson that outlived the winter: toy demos that work in the lab routinely fail to scale to the messy real world.

    5. AI returns as a business. Expert systems — thousands of hand-written IF–THEN rules capturing a specialist’s knowledge — genuinely work in narrow domains: MYCIN suggests antibiotic doses, XCON configures DEC computers and saves the company millions per year. Japan launches the Fifth Generation project; corporations build “knowledge engineering” departments.

    6. Expert systems hit their ceiling: rules are expensive to write, brittle at the edges, and a nightmare to maintain as the world changes. The specialized hardware market collapses, the Fifth Generation project winds down short of its goals, and “AI” becomes a word researchers avoid putting in grant applications — useful work continues under names like “machine learning” and “pattern recognition.”

    7. IBM’s Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov — a landmark for search: examining hundreds of millions of positions per second, guided by hand-tuned evaluation heuristics. Notably, it involved almost no learning. Chess fell to brute-force search plus human-crafted knowledge; the games machines couldn’t win that way (like Go) would need something else entirely.

    8. Machine learning quietly becomes the workhorse of the web: spam filters, search ranking, product recommendations, fraud detection, ad targeting. The winning recipe is data + statistics rather than hand-written rules. Three ingredients accumulate that will detonate the next era: the internet’s oceans of data, cheap parallel compute (GPUs), and steadily better learning algorithms.

    9. On the million-image ImageNet benchmark, a deep neural network called AlexNet — trained on two consumer GPUs — crushes the field, dropping the error rate by a margin the community had taken a decade to achieve incrementally. The message: given enough data and compute, networks that learn their own features beat systems built from hand-engineered ones. Vision, speech, and translation all fall in quick succession.

    10. Go has more board positions than atoms in the observable universe — brute-force search alone cannot crack it. DeepMind’s AlphaGo combines deep neural networks (to judge positions and suggest moves) with search and reinforcement learning (improving by playing itself), and defeats champion Lee Sedol 4–1. Move 37 of game two — a move human experts initially called a mistake — becomes shorthand for machine strategies no person taught.

    11. A Google paper titled Attention Is All You Need introduces the transformer — a neural network architecture that processes whole sequences in parallel and learns which parts of the input to “attend” to. It scales beautifully with data and compute, and becomes the substrate of essentially every modern language model, plus much of vision, audio, and biology AI.

    12. Scaling transformers into large language models (GPT-3, 2020) produces systems that write essays, code, and conversation. Diffusion models turn text prompts into photorealistic images. When chat interfaces put these models in everyone’s hands in late 2022, AI stops being an industry tool and becomes a consumer phenomenon — reaching a hundred million users faster than any prior technology.

    13. Frontier models become multimodal — one system reads text, sees images, hears audio — and increasingly agentic: using tools, browsing, writing and running code, taking multi-step actions toward a goal. Alongside the capability race runs a parallel effort on evaluation, safety, and alignment — because deploying systems this capable responsibly is now an engineering discipline of its own.

    Why the boom–bust rhythm? Each boom was powered by a technique that genuinely worked — and each winter arrived when that technique’s ceiling was mistaken for a temporary hurdle. The current era differs in one measurable way: modern methods have kept improving as data and compute grow, and they generate revenue at scale, which past booms never did. Whether today’s techniques have their own ceiling — and where it is — is one of the field’s genuinely open questions.

    04 The first way

    Rule-based AI: intelligence as logic

    The earliest working AI systems encoded a human expert’s knowledge as explicit IF–THEN rules. An inference engine matches your facts against the rules, fires the ones that apply, adds their conclusions as new facts, and repeats — chaining rules together until it reaches a decision. No statistics, no training data: pure logic, written by hand.

    Try the miniature triage advisor below — a toy cousin of MYCIN, the 1970s system that recommended antibiotic treatments. Toggle the facts a patient reports, run the engine, and watch rules fire and chain. Then press “add a curveball” to meet the architecture’s famous weakness.

    Reported facts

    Rule base

        A deliberately tiny toy — real expert systems held thousands of rules, and real medicine belongs to doctors. The teaching point is the mechanism: facts in, rules fire, conclusions chain.

        Why rules were loved

        • Explainable — the system can print exactly which rules fired and why. Auditors, doctors, and regulators can read the reasoning line by line.
        • Predictable — the same facts always produce the same answer. No randomness, no drift.
        • Reliable in narrow domains — where the world fits the rulebook (tax codes, equipment configuration, eligibility checks), they still quietly run today.

        Why they hit a ceiling

        • Brittle — one fact outside the rulebook and the system doesn’t degrade gracefully; it simply has nothing to say (you just saw this).
        • Hard to scale — every rule is written, tested, and maintained by hand, and rules interact in exploding combinations.
        • Knowledge is tacit — experts recognize things they cannot articulate as rules. Try writing IF–THEN conditions that recognize a cat in a photo.

        That last bullet is the pivot for everything that follows: if experts can’t write the rules down, maybe the machine should learn the rules from examples instead. Hold that thought through the next chapter — first, one classical technique earned a permanent place in the toolbox.

        05 The classical workhorse

        Search & planning: exploring possibilities

        Many problems share one shape: a starting state, a goal, and a space of possible moves between them. Routes on a map, chess positions, puzzle states, robot motions, airline schedules. Search is the art of exploring that space without drowning in it — and it remains one of AI’s most-used techniques, seventy years on.

        The maze below is a state space you can see. Choose a strategy and run it: breadth-first search ripples outward and guarantees the shortest path but explores enormously; depth-first search commits to one corridor at a time, cheap on memory but blind to path quality; greedy search chases whatever looks closest to the goal and can be fooled; A* combines the distance already traveled with a heuristic estimate of the distance remaining — the best of both, and the algorithm inside every navigation app. Click the maze to add or remove walls.

        Planning is search’s ambitious sibling: instead of one path through a maze, find a sequence of actions that transforms the world from its current state into a goal state — the mathematics behind logistics scheduling, factory automation, and robot task planning. Deep Blue beat Kasparov with search; AlphaGo paired search with learned intuition. The technique never left — it just gained partners.

        06 The great shift

        Machine learning: programs that write their own rules

        Machine learning inverts the rule-based recipe. Instead of a human writing rules and the machine applying them to data, the human supplies data plus the right answers, and a learning algorithm finds the rule — a model — that connects them. The model is then used to make predictions about examples it has never seen.

        Labeled examples — emails marked spam or not, photos with captions, past loans with outcomes.

        Model

        Two lessons hide here. First: the algorithm never knew what a cat is — it found a statistical boundary, nothing more. Second: feed it mislabeled or unrepresentative data and it learns the errors just as faithfully. Every headline about AI bias is this picture at scale.

        Why was this shift so consequential? Because data is abundant where expertise is scarce. Companies had millions of labeled examples — transactions marked fraudulent, emails marked spam — long before anyone could write rules covering them. Machine learning turned data itself into the programmer. Its limit: with simple models, humans still had to hand-design the measurements (the features). Choosing “weight” and “ear length” above was feature engineering — and for images, audio, and language, nobody could design features good enough. That is the wall deep learning broke.

        07 Layers upon layers

        Deep learning: features that learn themselves

        A neural network is a stack of simple units, each computing a weighted sum of its inputs and passing the result on. Stack enough layers and something remarkable happens: the network learns its own features. Early layers detect edges and textures; middle layers combine them into shapes and parts; late layers recognize whole objects. Nobody designs this hierarchy — it emerges from training on examples.

        A cartoon of the real thing — production vision models have millions of units, and their “features” are messier than edges → shapes → parts. But the principle shown — increasingly abstract features, learned not designed — is exactly why deep learning works.

        Three doors opened at once around 2012, and deep learning walked through all of them. Vision: networks that learn edges-to-objects hierarchies surpassed two decades of hand-crafted features almost overnight. Speech: recognition error rates fell so far that dictation became a default interface. Translation and language: instead of grammar rules, networks learned the statistics of how sentences correspond — and with the transformer architecture, how every word in a passage relates to every other. The recipe scaled with an appetite no previous technique had shown: more data, more layers, more compute kept yielding more capability.

        That appetite is why this story is also a hardware story. Training a network means millions of small multiplications that can all happen at once — exactly the workload graphics processors were built for. Feeding those processors means moving oceans of training data through the memory hierarchy, and training the largest models means renting thousands of cloud machines that work in concert. Deep learning didn’t just borrow the computer’s infrastructure — it bent the industry’s roadmap around itself.

        08 Learning by doing

        Reinforcement learning: trial, error, reward

        Nobody labels examples for a toddler learning to walk. The child acts, the world responds, and falling over teaches. Reinforcement learning follows the same loop: an agent takes an action in an environment, receives a reward or a penalty, and updates its behavior to earn more reward over time. No answer key — just consequences.

        The robot below knows nothing: not where the charging station is, not that the pits are dangerous, not even what “forward” means. It learns a value for every move in every cell purely from rewards (+10 for the charger, −10 for a pit, a tiny cost for each step). Early on it wanders randomly — that’s exploration. As estimates firm up, it increasingly exploits what it knows. Watch the arrows: they are the learned policy, and they visibly improve.

        09 The new chapter

        Generative AI: from predicting to creating

        Every model so far predicted an existing label: spam or not, cat or dog, which move is best. Generative AI turns prediction into creation: predict the next word, then the next, then the next — and a paragraph appears that never existed before. The same trick with pixels, audio samples, or code tokens yields images, voices, and software.

        Traditional prediction

        Input → Model → one of N known labels

        “Is this review positive or negative?” The output space is small and fixed. The model chooses.

        Generative AI

        Input → Model → new content, piece by piece

        “Write a review of this cafe.” The output space is effectively infinite. The model composes — one predicted fragment at a time.

        The demo below is a deliberately tiny autoregressive text generator — it picks each next word from probabilities learned from a few paragraphs, where a large language model picks each next token using billions of parameters trained on much of the written internet. What it shares with the real thing is the loop: look at the text so far, form a probability over what comes next (watch the bars), sample one option, repeat. The temperature slider is the same control real systems expose: low temperature always takes the top choice (safe, repetitive); high temperature gambles on long shots (creative, and quickly incoherent).

        Not all generation is next-piece prediction. Diffusion models — behind most AI image tools — work in reverse: start from pure noise and remove it step by step, steering each step toward the text prompt, until an image condenses out. Drag the slider to walk the denoising process yourself.

        step 0 — pure noise

        Multimodal systems close the loop: one model maps text, images, and audio into a shared internal representation, so it can describe a photo, answer questions about a chart, or turn a sketch into code. Autoregression, diffusion, multimodality — different mechanisms, one idea: learn the structure of a medium deeply enough and you can produce new examples of it.

        11 The honest chapter

        What AI can’t do (yet, or at all)

        Every technique on this page has sharp edges, and pretending otherwise is how AI gets misused. None of these limitations cancels the capabilities you’ve seen — and none of the capabilities cancels these limitations. Both lists are true at once.

        A language model is trained to produce plausible continuations, and truth is only correlated with plausibility. When the model lacks knowledge, it doesn’t fall silent — it produces something that sounds right, with the same confident tone either way: invented citations, plausible-but-wrong numbers, confident nonsense. Mitigations (retrieving real documents, citing sources, tool use) reduce the rate substantially; nothing yet eliminates it. Rule of thumb: the more checkable a claim, the more it should be checked.

        You watched the perceptron in chapter 6 learn mislabeled points without complaint. Scale that up: models trained on historical hiring, lending, or policing data learn the historical patterns — including the unjust ones — and then apply them at machine speed with a veneer of objectivity. Countermeasures exist (auditing, balanced datasets, fairness constraints) but they are engineering disciplines, not checkboxes, and they require knowing what to look for.

        “Garbage in, garbage out” predates AI by decades and governs it completely. Models interpolate brilliantly within their training distribution and degrade — sometimes silently — outside it. A medical imaging model trained at one hospital can stumble on another hospital’s scanners; a fraud model decays as fraudsters adapt. Much of production “AI engineering” is actually data engineering and monitoring for drift.

        The perceptron never knew what a cat is; the same caution applies up the stack. Models can manipulate symbols impressively while lacking grounded models of the world — failing at simple physical reasoning, arithmetic edge cases, or novel situations a child would handle. Where the line sits between “very deep pattern matching” and “understanding” is a live scientific debate; that the systems fail differently than humans do is not.

        Modern models plan and reason far better than their critics expected and far worse than their marketing implies — often both in the same session. Performance drops on problems requiring many rigorous steps, exact constraint tracking, or knowing when to say “this is unsolvable.” Techniques like chain-of-thought reasoning, tool use, and verification loops help materially, which is precisely why serious deployments wrap models in scaffolding rather than trusting a single raw response.

        Training a frontier model consumes megawatt-scale power for months across thousands of accelerators, and serving millions of users costs real energy per response. The industry response — more efficient chips, smaller specialized models, on-device inference — is genuine, but so is the trend line: capability has so far scaled with compute. Efficiency and capability are now the field’s twin engineering races.

        Models are trained on data, remember statistical traces of it, and receive whatever users type into them — three separate privacy surfaces. Good deployments treat each one deliberately: minimizing and anonymizing training data, encrypting data in transit and at rest, and setting clear retention rules for user inputs. The techniques exist; applying them is a choice that has to be made, not assumed.

        Every limitation above shares one mitigation: a human who stays responsible. High-stakes uses — medicine, law, lending, safety systems — work best with AI as a drafting and screening layer whose outputs humans review, and worst as an unsupervised decision maker. This isn’t AI pessimism; it’s the same discipline engineering applies to any powerful component with known failure modes.

        12 Open frontiers

        Where the field is heading

        Prediction has a poor track record in AI — both the “solved in a decade” camp and the “impossible in principle” camp have been embarrassed repeatedly. What follows are not forecasts but active research directions: places where real work, real money, and real open problems currently live.

        Agents that act

        From answering questions to completing tasks: systems that browse, write and run code, use tools, and carry out multi-step work with checkpoints. The open problems are reliability over long chains of actions and knowing when to stop and ask.

        Robotics’ second act

        Learned models are replacing scripted motions — robots that acquire manipulation skills from demonstration and trial. The gap between a lab demo and a kitchen remains wide; the gap is narrowing measurably.

        Scientific discovery

        Protein structure prediction already reshaped biology. Materials discovery, weather modeling, and drug design are following — AI as an instrument for science, with verification still owned by experiment.

        Personalized education

        A patient tutor for every learner, adapting pace and explanation style — the oldest dream in educational technology, newly plausible. The open questions are pedagogy and honest measurement of outcomes, not interface polish.

        Healthcare with oversight

        Imaging triage, documentation relief, and literature synthesis are deploying now — the pattern that works keeps clinicians deciding while AI drafts and flags. Regulation and validation pipelines are maturing alongside.

        On-device AI

        Small, efficient models running on phones and laptops — private by physics, instant by proximity. The craft: distilling large-model capability into a footprint measured in gigabytes of RAM, not racks.

        Multimodal everything

        Text, vision, audio, and action converging into single models that perceive and communicate across media. The research edge: keeping grounding and reliability while the input space explodes.

        Human–AI collaboration

        Interfaces where people steer and machines amplify — pair programming, drafting, design exploration. The measurable goal is not replacing judgment but compressing the distance between intent and result.

        Safety & alignment

        Making systems honest, controllable, and steerable as capability grows — evaluation science, interpretability (opening the black box), and alignment techniques. Less a brake on the field than the engineering that lets it ship.

        13 Capstone

        AI Explorer: match the problem to the approach

        You now know five families of techniques. Real engineering is choosing among them. Pick a problem, set the constraints you’re working under, and see how the fit of each approach shifts — then read why. There is rarely one right answer; there are always trade-offs.

        Scores are didactic, not benchmarks — they encode the rules of thumb practitioners actually use: rules and search when the problem is crisply specifiable, learning when it isn’t; simple models when data is scarce or answers must be explained; deep learning when perception or language is involved and data is plentiful.
