01 The big picture

What is the Internet, actually?

The Internet is not one giant computer, and it isn’t a cloud. It is billions of independent devices — laptops, phones, servers, routers — that can all exchange data because they agree on a shared set of rules called protocols. Nobody owns it; everybody who follows the rules can join it. The name literally means inter-network: a network of networks.

Your home Wi-Fi is one small network. Your Internet Service Provider (ISP) runs a much bigger one. Long-haul backbone operators run continent-spanning fiber networks, and cloud providers run networks of data centers. The Internet is what you get when all of these voluntarily interconnect. Watch the request below leave a laptop and cross each of those networks in turn — and notice the response making the same trip in reverse. This exact chain reappears in every chapter that follows.

Simplified on purpose: a real request may cross 10–30 routers, and the “regional network” and “backbone” stages are often several distinct companies. The structure — small network, bigger network, biggest networks, destination — is accurate.

02 Names to numbers

DNS: finding the address

Computers route traffic by number, not by name. Before your browser can send a single byte to www.google.com, it must learn that machine’s IP address. The Domain Name System (DNS) is the globally distributed directory that answers that question — often called the “phonebook of the Internet,” though the analogy undersells it: this phonebook is split across millions of servers, cached everywhere, and updated constantly.

The lookup is a chain of delegation. Your device asks a recursive resolver (usually run by your ISP, or a public one like 1.1.1.1 or 8.8.8.8). If the resolver doesn’t already know the answer, it asks a root server, which doesn’t know either — but knows who handles .com. The TLD server for .com knows who speaks for google.com: its authoritative servers, which finally return the IP. Step through it below.

    03 Numbers with structure

    IP addresses: where, not who

    DNS handed back 142.250.72.196. An IP address is not a serial number — it is a location in the network’s structure, more like a postal address than a name. The left part identifies a network; the right part identifies a device on it. That structure is what lets routers forward traffic without knowing every device on Earth.

    IPv4 addresses are 32 bits — four numbers from 0 to 255 — giving about 4.3 billion possibilities, which ran out years ago. IPv6 is the 128-bit successor with room for every device, sensor, and lightbulb ever made. The shortage is why your home devices use private addresses (like 192.168.x.x) that never appear on the public Internet: your router performs NAT (Network Address Translation), letting a whole household share one public address.     04 Hop by hop

    Routers & ISPs: no map, just neighbors

    No single machine knows the whole route from your laptop to Google. Instead, each router keeps a routing table — a list of “for addresses like X, hand the packet to neighbor Y” — and every packet is forwarded one hop at a time. The route emerges from thousands of local decisions, which is exactly why the Internet survives failures: when a link dies, neighbors update their tables and traffic flows around the hole.

    Your packet typically crosses your home router, your ISP’s local equipment, a regional ISP network, one or more backbone providers, and finally the destination’s own network. (Inside each building, switches do the short-range equivalent: forwarding frames between machines on the same network, while routers forward between networks.) Try it below: send packets, then click a router mid-stream to knock it offline, or add congestion, and watch the network route around the damage.

    Real routing is driven by protocols (BGP between networks, OSPF and friends inside them) and by business relationships, not just distance. The behavior shown — multiple paths, automatic rerouting, congestion making a longer path worth taking — is the honest core of it.

    05 The unit of the Internet

    Packets: everything is chopped up

    The Internet never sends a web page. It sends packets — small chunks, typically up to about 1,500 bytes each — and every packet travels independently. A modest page becomes hundreds of them; they may take different routes, arrive out of order, or not arrive at all, and the receiving end reassembles the original from sequence numbers.

    This design choice, packet switching, is the founding idea of the Internet. There is no reserved circuit between you and Google the way old telephone lines reserved a wire; links are shared by everyone, packet by packet, which is what makes the network efficient and resilient.     06 Two philosophies

    TCP vs UDP: certainty or speed

    Packets get lost — congestion, interference, overloaded routers. What happens next depends on the transport protocol. TCP promises complete, in-order delivery: every packet is acknowledged, and anything lost is retransmitted. UDP promises nothing: packets are fired off and whatever arrives, arrives — but with zero waiting.

    Neither is “better.” A file download with one missing chunk is corrupt, so downloads and web pages use TCP. A video call packet that arrives late is worthless — that moment already happened — so real-time audio, video, and games ride on UDP and simply tolerate small gaps. (Modern HTTP/3 gets the best of both: it runs QUIC over UDP and rebuilds reliability inside it.) Raise the packet loss and watch each protocol keep its own promise:

    07 The padlock

    HTTPS & TLS: talking in private

    Your packets cross networks owned by strangers. Plain HTTP sends readable text — anyone along the path could read or alter it. HTTPS is the same HTTP wrapped in TLS (Transport Layer Security), which does two jobs before any page data flows: proves you’re talking to the real google.com, and agrees on secret keys so everything after is encrypted.

    The proof comes from a certificate — a signed statement from a certificate authority your browser already trusts that says “this public key belongs to google.com.” The secrecy comes from a key exchange: both sides derive the same session key without ever sending it across the wire.       08 Moving content closer

      CDNs: the speed of light is the law

      Light in fiber covers about 200,000 km per second — fast, but finite. Sydney to Virginia and back is unavoidably ~200 ms per round trip, and loading a page takes several. No software fixes physics. The fix is geographic: a Content Delivery Network keeps copies of static content — images, scripts, video — in hundreds of edge locations worldwide, so your request travels to a nearby city instead of another continent.

      Pick where you’re browsing from. The origin server stays in Virginia; toggle the CDN and compare the trip. This is why video streaming, software updates, and image-heavy sites feel local everywhere: the first viewer near you warms the cache, everyone after is served from around the corner.

      Latencies are speed-of-light-in-fiber estimates over great-circle distances, roughly doubled for real cable paths and equipment delays — representative, not measured. Dynamic, personalized content still travels to the origin (or to compute running at the edge).

      09 Sharing the load

      Load balancers: one name, many servers

      Millions of people are “on google.com” right now, and no single server could survive that. Behind the name stands a fleet, and in front of the fleet stands a load balancer: it accepts every incoming request and forwards each one to a healthy, not-too-busy server. Servers can fail, join, or be replaced without visitors ever noticing.

      Crank the traffic below. With autoscaling on, new servers spin up when load climbs and drain away when it falls — the same elasticity story told in the Understanding Cloud Computing exhibit. Watch what the load balancer does when a server goes red: it simply stops sending traffic there.

      10 Where the servers live

      Data centers: the Internet’s buildings

      “The cloud” is a building — many buildings: windowless warehouses holding tens of thousands of servers stacked in racks, wired together by layers of switches, fed by redundant power, and cooled around the clock. Your request’s final hops happen inside one of these rooms, over cables a few meters long.

      12 Performance

      Why some websites are fast

      Page speed is a budget with several line items: name lookup, connection setup, server thinking time, bytes on the wire, and rendering. Latency (distance and round trips) and bandwidth (bytes per second) are different costs — a fat pipe doesn’t shorten a long round trip. Fast sites attack every line: move content closer, send fewer bytes, cache aggressively.

      A didactic model, not a benchmark: real loads overlap these phases, open multiple connections, and depend on device speed. The shape is right — distance multiplies every round trip, the CDN shrinks distance, compression shrinks bytes, and a warm cache skips most of the bill.

      13 Setting the record straight

      Common misconceptions

      Seven things people believe about the Internet that aren’t quite true — each one a quick expand.

      The Internet and the Web are the same thing.

      The Internet is the transport: the global network moving packets between machines. The World Wide Web is one application riding on it — pages, links, HTTP. Email, video calls, online games, and app backends all use the Internet without being the Web.

      Wi-Fi is the Internet.

      Wi-Fi is only the last few meters: a radio link between your device and your router. If your ISP goes down, your Wi-Fi stays “connected” while nothing loads — because the wireless hop was never the Internet, just the on-ramp to it.

      DNS hosts websites.

      DNS stores only small typed records — chiefly name-to-address mappings. The website itself lives on web servers elsewhere. DNS is the directory that tells your browser where to go; it never serves the page.

      Data always takes the shortest path.

      Routing follows routing tables, which follow business agreements between networks as much as geography. Traffic between two buildings in the same city can detour through another city entirely because that’s where the two ISPs exchange traffic. Congestion and failures reroute it further.

      HTTPS means a website is trustworthy.

      The padlock proves two things: you reached the domain shown, and nobody between you can read or alter the traffic. A phishing site can have a perfectly valid certificate — the connection to the scammer is securely encrypted. HTTPS authenticates the pipe, not the character of who’s on the other end.

      Download speed is the only thing that matters.

      Bandwidth is how much data flows per second; latency is how long each round trip takes. Loading a page requires several round trips before bulk data even starts, and video calls live or die on latency. Past a comfortable threshold, extra megabits change little — distance and round trips are usually the real bottleneck.

      The cloud is separate from the Internet.

      “The cloud” is data centers reached over the ordinary Internet, run by companies that rent out computing. Your cloud photos sit on physical drives in a building; the same DNS, routing, and TLS from this page carry every byte to and from them.
