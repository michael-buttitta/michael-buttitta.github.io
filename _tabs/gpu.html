01 The problem

Why GPUs exist

A CPU is built to finish one thing as fast as possible. But a 3D scene needs the same small computation repeated for millions of pixels — and none of those pixels needs to wait for the others. That is a different problem, and it deserved a different machine.

CPU

8 cores · one task each

Finished: 0 / 256 · 0.0 ms

GPU

Hundreds of lanes at once

Finished: 0 / 256 · 0.0 ms

Each GPU lane is slower and simpler than a CPU core — it wins on volume, not speed. That is the whole trade, and every chapter below is a consequence of it.

How CPUs and GPUs differ
CPUGPU
Cores A few powerful cores (8–24) Thousands of lightweight cores
Optimized for Latency — finish this task now Throughput — finish all tasks soon
Control logic Branch prediction, out-of-order execution, speculation Simple in-order lanes; stalls hidden by switching threads
Ideal workloads Branch-heavy, serial logic: operating systems, databases, general computing Massive parallel math: graphics, AI, scientific computing
A cache miss costs A stalled program Nothing visible — thousands of other threads keep the chip busy

02 The silicon

Inside the GPU

A GPU is not one processor — it is a small city of specialized blocks. Select any component to see what it does, why it exists, and who it talks to.

Stylized die — block sizes are illustrative, not to scale.

Every block exists to keep one number high: useful work completed per second per watt. The tour starts wherever you click.

Zoom in: one Streaming Multiprocessor

PCIe interface

The GPU's front door

The link to the CPU and system RAM. A PCIe 5.0 x16 slot moves about 64 GB/s each way — commands, textures, and model weights all arrive through it.

A discrete GPU is a co-processor: without a host link it never receives work. But 64 GB/s is a trickle next to VRAM's terabytes per second, which is why data is copied into VRAM once and kept there.

Receives command buffers from the driver; DMA engines stream data through the memory controllers into VRAM.

VRAM

GDDR7 on consumer cards · HBM stacks on data-center parts

Dedicated graphics memory surrounding the die. It holds everything the GPU works on: textures, meshes, framebuffers, and AI model weights, at 1–2+ TB/s of aggregate bandwidth.

Thousands of cores consume data faster than any CPU-style memory system could serve it. Capacity is a hard cliff, too: spill past it and data pages over PCIe at 1/30th the speed.

Served by the on-die memory controllers; the L2 cache absorbs its hottest lines; the display engine scans the finished frame straight out of it.

Memory controllers

The traffic engineers

On-die partitions that schedule every read and write to the GDDR chips, reordering and batching requests to keep each memory channel saturated.

DRAM rewards sequential, grouped access. Smart scheduling here is the difference between using 60% and 95% of the bus you paid for.

Sits between the L2 slices and the VRAM chips. When a warp's 32 loads are coalesced (Chapter 08), they arrive here as a handful of transactions instead of 32.

L2 cache

AMD adds a larger L3: "Infinity Cache"

A pool of on-die SRAM — tens of megabytes — shared by every SM. The last stop before a request pays the full price of VRAM.

A hit here is several times faster and far cheaper in energy than DRAM. Depth buffers, reused tiles, and neighboring threads' data live here for free.

Backs every SM's L1; misses flow to the memory controllers. The raster engine's blends also land here on their way to the framebuffer.

Command front end

NVIDIA: GigaThread Engine · AMD: command processor + ACEs

Reads command buffers written by the driver, chops the work into thread blocks (workgroups), and deals them out to SMs the moment slots free up.

A hundred-plus SMs are useless if any of them sits idle. Global scheduling and load-balancing keeps the whole chip saturated.

Fed by the PCIe interface; dispatches to every SM; hands raster output back to SMs as fragment-shading work.

Raster engine

Includes the ROPs (render output units)

Fixed-function triangle hardware: clips and culls, walks each triangle to find the pixels it covers, and emits fragments. Its ROP partners run depth tests and blend final colors into the framebuffer.

Rasterization is so regular and so relentless that dedicated silicon beats programmable cores at it by orders of magnitude, in both speed and energy.

Takes assembled triangles from vertex shading on the SMs; sends fragments back to the SMs; ROPs write through L2 to VRAM.

Display engine

Scanout hardware

Reads the finished framebuffer and streams it to your monitor — resolutions, refresh rates, HDR, and variable refresh (G-Sync / FreeSync) all live here.

Scanout is hard real-time: a missed deadline is a visible glitch. A dedicated block never misses, no matter how busy the rest of the chip is.

Reads from VRAM on a fixed clock; drives the DisplayPort / HDMI links.

Streaming Multiprocessor (SM)

AMD: Compute Unit (CU) / dual-CU WGP

The GPU's repeating worker block: warp schedulers, a big register file, 128 shader cores, tensor cores, an RT core, texture units, and shared memory. A flagship die carries 100–170 of them.

GPUs scale by stamping out identical SMs and wiring them to a shared L2 — more silicon budget, more SMs, more parallel throughput.

Receives thread blocks from the command front end; reads and writes through its L1 to the shared L2 and VRAM. Zoom in below to see inside one.

Shader cores

NVIDIA: CUDA cores · AMD: stream processors

The scalar FP32 / INT32 arithmetic units — 128 per SM — that execute one warp instruction across 32 threads in lockstep.

They are the general-purpose muscle: vertex math, pixel shading, physics, any parallel arithmetic that isn't a matrix multiply or a ray hit.

Issued by the warp schedulers, operands from the register file; loads go through L1 / shared memory, then L2.

Tensor cores

AMD: Matrix cores / WMMA instructions

Matrix engines that multiply-accumulate an entire small tile (e.g. 16×16) per operation in FP16, BF16, FP8 or INT8 — accumulating in FP32 to keep results stable.

Deep learning is nearly all matrix multiplication. Doing it as one fused hardware operation is 10–30× the per-clock throughput of the shader cores. Chapter 06 shows it live.

Shares the SM's schedulers and register file with the shader cores — it is an execution unit, not a separate chip region.

RT cores

AMD: Ray Accelerators

Fixed-function ray units that walk BVH trees (the scene's spatial index) and test ray-vs-box and ray-vs-triangle intersections in hardware.

Finding what a ray hits is branchy pointer-chasing that wastes shader cores. Casting it into silicon is what made real-time ray tracing (Chapter 07) possible.

Shader programs fire rays at it and get hit results back; the material shading of each hit still runs on the shader cores.

Texture units

TMUs

Fetch texels, decompress GPU texture formats, pick mip levels, and run bilinear / trilinear / anisotropic filtering — all in hardware, one instruction from the shader's point of view.

A single filtered sample would otherwise cost dozens of ALU operations and multiple scattered loads. Games issue billions of samples per second.

Read through a dedicated texture path in L1, then L2, then VRAM.

L1 / shared memory

AMD: LDS (local data share)

~128 KB of on-SM storage that is both an L1 cache and a software-managed scratchpad. Threads in the same block cooperate through it explicitly.

Data a tile will reuse — say a block of a matrix — gets fetched from VRAM once instead of 32 times. It is the single most important tool for beating the bandwidth wall.

A few cycles from the shader cores; refills from L2 on miss.

Warp schedulers

Four per SM

Every clock, each scheduler looks at its dozens of resident warps and issues the next instruction from one that is ready — skipping any warp stalled on memory.

This is the GPU's latency-hiding trick. A memory wait that would stall a CPU core simply means a different warp runs. The math units barely notice.

Reads warp state from the register file; drives the shader and tensor cores.

Register file

256 KB per SM

Fast storage where every resident thread keeps its variables — permanently, for the thread's whole life. No saving or restoring on a switch.

Zero-cost warp switching only works because every warp's state is already on-chip. The budget is real, though: greedy kernels that use many registers per thread reduce how many warps fit (Chapter 08).

Feeds operands to shader and tensor cores under the schedulers' control.

04 The classic job

Rendering a frame

Every frame of every game crosses this assembly line — typically in under 16 milliseconds. Select a stage to see the same tiny scene at that point in its life, or play the whole pipeline.

Vertex processing

Every vertex of every model runs a small program — the vertex shader — that transforms it from 3D object space into screen coordinates. Thousands of vertices are processed in parallel across the SMs.

Transformed vertices are grouped back into triangles. Triangles facing away from the camera are culled and off-screen ones are clipped — a huge share of the scene is discarded here, nearly for free.

Fixed-function hardware walks each surviving triangle and answers one question: which pixels does it cover? Each covered pixel becomes a fragment, its attributes (depth, UVs, normal) interpolated from the triangle's corners.

Every fragment runs the fragment shader: sample textures through the texture units, evaluate the material. At 4K that can be 8+ million fragments — usually the heaviest stage of the frame.

Surfaces meet lights: N·L diffuse math, specular highlights, shadow-map lookups. Forward renderers do this inside the fragment shader; deferred renderers replay it as a separate full-screen pass over the G-buffer.

The finished image is graded and polished: tone mapping, bloom, anti-aliasing — and increasingly an AI upscaler (DLSS / FSR) that renders fewer pixels and reconstructs the rest on the tensor cores.

Fragments pass the depth test and are blended by the ROPs into the framebuffer in VRAM. When possible, early-Z runs the depth test before shading, discarding hidden fragments without paying for them.

The display engine scans the completed buffer out over DisplayPort or HDMI, line by line. Double buffering plus vsync or variable refresh keeps a half-drawn frame from ever reaching the glass.

05 The supply chain

VRAM & memory

Compute is only half the machine. The other half is logistics: keeping thousands of cores fed. Here is what actually occupies a 24 GB card — and why the speed of that memory matters more than its size.

24 GB GDDR7

Select a segment of the memory map.

Capacity is a cliff. Bandwidth is a ceiling.

Run out of capacity and data spills over PCIe at ~1/30th the speed — performance falls off a cliff. But long before that, bandwidth caps you: if a task reads more bytes than the bus can deliver, the cores idle no matter how many you have. An LLM generating one token must read every weight once — that is why inference speed tracks memory bandwidth almost linearly.

Latency is hidden, not eliminated

A VRAM access costs hundreds of clock cycles. The GPU's answer is the cache ladder below — and, when that fails, the warp switch from Chapter 08: while one warp waits, others compute. Latency is still there; it is simply somebody else's problem.

Bandwidth, visualized

VRAM to SMs: ~1,800 GB/s on a flagship card. PCIe to system RAM: ~64 GB/s. The dense stream is why data lives in VRAM; the thin one is why running out of VRAM hurts.

06 The new job

How GPUs power AI

Strip a neural network to its skeleton and you find one operation repeated billions of times: multiply a row by a column, add it up. Matrix multiplication. It happens to be the most parallel workload ever put in front of a computer.

A (activations)

×

B (weights)

=

C (output)

Press play: each output cell is one row of A dotted with one column of B.

Multiply-accumulates0

A shader core does these multiply-accumulates one at a time. A tensor core consumes an entire tile of this matrix in a single operation — and a big model is nothing but tiles. One generated LLM token is roughly 2 × (parameter count) operations: ~14 billion of these for a 7B model. Per token.

Mixed precision: smaller numbers, faster math

Neural networks tolerate low-precision arithmetic remarkably well, so tensor cores compute in FP16, FP8 — even FP4 — while accumulating sums in FP32 so error doesn't compound. Halving the bits roughly doubles the math rate and halves the bytes moved.

FP32 · shader cores1×
FP16 · tensor cores~15×
FP8 · tensor cores~30×

Dense-matrix throughput relative to FP32 shader ALUs, H100-class hardware. Vendor slides often double these again by assuming "structured sparsity" — read the footnotes.

Training

Run the network forward, measure the error, run it backward to compute gradients, update every weight. Roughly 3× the compute of a forward pass, plus optimizer state that multiplies memory use. Modern frontier models train on tens of thousands of GPUs for months — the workload that made GPUs the world's most valuable silicon.

Inference

Forward pass only: weights are frozen, the job is answering fast and cheap. Generating each token means reading every weight from VRAM once — so inference lives and dies on memory bandwidth and clever batching, while training leans harder on raw compute.

Why GPUs beat CPUs at deep learning

  • The shape fits. Matrix multiplies are millions of independent multiply-adds — exactly what thousands of lanes want.
  • Dedicated silicon. Tensor cores turn the inner loop into a single hardware instruction; CPUs have nothing comparable at scale.
  • Bandwidth. HBM feeds terabytes per second; a CPU socket delivers a tenth of that.
  • The software moat. CUDA, cuDNN, PyTorch and a decade of kernels mean the fast path is also the easy path.

08 The machinery

Architecture deep dive

Four small machines explain most of GPU performance: lockstep execution, divergence, occupancy, and coalescing. Master these and spec sheets start making sense.

SIMT: one instruction, 32 threads

Threads execute in fixed lockstep groups — warps of 32 on NVIDIA, wavefronts of 32/64 on AMD. One instruction is issued; every thread in the group runs it on its own data. Branches are handled by masking: if threads disagree, the warp runs both paths, muting half each time.

if (value > 0) { pathA(); } else { pathB(); }

Occupancy: how many warps fit

Every resident warp keeps its registers on-chip. Ask for more registers per thread and fewer warps fit — fewer warps means less latency hiding. Drag the slider:

64

Memory coalescing

When a warp loads memory, the hardware merges the 32 addresses into as few 32-byte transactions as possible. Neighboring threads reading neighboring addresses = one cache line. Strided access = 32 separate transactions for the same useful data.

SIMD vs SIMT

SIMD (CPUs): one thread drives a wide register — "add these 8 floats to those 8 floats." The programmer manages lanes, edges, and masks explicitly.

SIMT (GPUs): you write a program for one thread; the hardware runs 32 copies in lockstep and handles the masking. Same silicon idea — radically better ergonomics, which is a real reason GPU computing took off.

Under the hood a warp instruction is a 32-lane SIMD operation — SIMT is the programming model on top.

09 The spec sheet

Reading a spec sheet

Every number below is real, and every one of them is routinely misread. Expand a metric for what it means, why it matters, and the trap. Values shown are flagship-class, 2025, for scale.

CUDA / shader cores~20,000+

Definition. The count of scalar FP32 ALUs across all SMs.

Why it matters. First-order proxy for parallel math capacity within one architecture generation.

Misconception. Counts are not comparable across vendors or generations — a "core" is whatever the marketing department says it is. Compare benchmarks, not core counts.

Clock speed~2.5 GHz

Definition. How many cycles per second the cores run; boost clocks vary with thermals and load.

Why it matters. Throughput = lanes × ops/clock × clock. It is a straight multiplier.

Misconception. GPU clocks are deliberately lower than CPU clocks — wide and slow wins on performance per watt, which is the budget that actually binds.

TFLOPS (FP32)~80–100

Definition. Trillions of floating-point operations per second, assuming every lane does a fused multiply-add every clock.

Why it matters. The theoretical ceiling on compute-bound work.

Misconception. It is a peak, not a promise — real workloads hit a fraction of it, usually because memory can't keep the lanes fed (see the playground below).

Tensor TFLOPS~1,000–4,000

Definition. Matrix-multiply throughput of the tensor cores at reduced precision (FP16/FP8/FP4).

Why it matters. The number that predicts AI training and inference capability.

Misconception. Always check the precision and whether the figure assumes 2:4 structured sparsity — that footnote doubles the number for a feature most models don't use.

RT performancevendor units

Definition. Ray-triangle / ray-box intersection and BVH traversal rate of the RT cores, quoted in vendor-specific units ("RT TFLOPS", "gigarays").

Why it matters. Sets how much ray budget a frame gets before the denoiser has to work miracles.

Misconception. RT numbers from different vendors are not the same unit. Shading the hits still costs shader-core time — RT cores only find the hits.

VRAM capacity16–32 GB

Definition. How much data can live in graphics memory at once.

Why it matters. Textures at 4K, or model weights: it is a hard fit-or-don't-fit test. A 7B-parameter model in FP16 needs ~14 GB before you type a word.

Misconception. More VRAM does not make anything faster — until you run out, at which point nothing else matters.

Memory bandwidth~1–2 TB/s

Definition. Bytes per second the VRAM bus can move: bus width × memory clock × data rate.

Why it matters. The most under-appreciated spec on the sheet — LLM token rate and 4K frame rate both track it closely.

Misconception. TFLOPS get the headlines, but most real workloads are bandwidth-bound. When in doubt, this number is the bottleneck.

Cache size~64–128 MB

Definition. On-die L2 (NVIDIA) or L3 "Infinity Cache" (AMD) shared by all SMs.

Why it matters. Every hit is a VRAM trip you didn't pay for — big caches let a narrower, cheaper memory bus punch above its weight.

Misconception. Cache helps re-used data (framebuffers, tiles). Streaming workloads that touch everything once — like LLM weights — blow straight through it.

Clock vs power350–600 W

Definition. Total graphics power (TGP): the board's power budget under load.

Why it matters. Power is the binding constraint of modern silicon — data centers are sited next to power plants for a reason. Perf-per-watt decides what is buildable.

Misconception. The last 10% of clock speed costs far more than 10% more power — voltage scales quadratically. Slightly slower, wider chips are the efficient shape.

Memory bus width256–512 bit

Definition. How many wires connect the die to its VRAM chips, in bits transferred per clock edge.

Why it matters. Bandwidth = bus width × memory speed. Wide buses are expensive board- and die-area, which is why cache sizes ballooned instead.

Misconception. A narrow bus is not automatically slow — judge the delivered GB/s (and cache) rather than the wire count alone.

10 How we got here

Three decades of GPUs

From a chip that could only draw textured triangles to the engine of the AI economy — scroll the timeline.

1996

Fixed-function 3D

3dfx Voodoo brings dedicated triangle hardware to consumers. Every effect is a hard-wired switch: the chip can do exactly what its designers anticipated, brilliantly, and nothing else.

1999

"The world's first GPU"

GeForce 256 pulls transform & lighting onto the chip and NVIDIA coins the term GPU. Geometry math leaves the CPU for good.

2001

Programmable shaders

GeForce 3 and DirectX 8 let developers write small programs per vertex and per pixel. Graphics stops being a fixed menu and becomes software.

2006

Unified shaders

G80 (GeForce 8800) merges vertex and pixel hardware into one pool of general-purpose cores — the direct ancestor of every modern SM.

2007

CUDA

NVIDIA exposes those cores to C programmers. GPGPU computing — physics, imaging, finance — turns the graphics card into a general parallel computer.

2012

AlexNet

A neural network trained on two consumer GTX 580s demolishes the ImageNet benchmark. Deep learning's GPU era — and arguably the modern AI boom — starts here.

2017

Tensor cores

Volta (V100) adds dedicated matrix engines and mixed-precision math. AI throughput per chip jumps an order of magnitude.

2018

RTX: real-time ray tracing

Turing adds RT cores for hardware BVH traversal plus AI denoising — traced shadows and reflections become playable at 60 fps.

2020

RT goes mainstream

Ampere doubles down and AMD's RDNA2 puts Ray Accelerators in the PS5 and Xbox Series X. Ray tracing stops being a tech demo.

2022

The AI turn

Hopper's FP8 transformer engine targets LLMs directly; RDNA3 ships the first chiplet gaming GPU. ChatGPT launches, and GPUs become the scarcest hardware on Earth.

2024+

The Blackwell era

Two reticle-limit dies act as one GPU; NVLink fuses 72 of them into a single rack-scale accelerator for trillion-parameter models. The "chip" is now the whole rack.
