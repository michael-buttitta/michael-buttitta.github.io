01 The problem

Money, trust, and the copy-paste problem

Money is a technology, and humanity has upgraded it many times: from favors to shells to gold to paper to database rows. Every upgrade traded one problem for another — and the digital era introduced the strangest problem of all: perfect copies.

A short history of money

  1. Prehistory

    Barter

    Trading goods directly works only when each side wants exactly what the other has — the double coincidence of wants. It doesn't scale past a village.

  2. ≈ 1200 BCE

    Commodity money

    Cowrie shells, salt, cattle: scarce, useful things become a shared measure of value. Money turns out to be a social agreement, not a particular object.

  3. ≈ 600 BCE

    Coinage

    Lydia mints standardized metal coins. Gold and silver win for durable reasons: scarce, divisible, portable, and nearly impossible to counterfeit at scale.

  4. ≈ 1000s CE

    Paper promises

    Song-dynasty China circulates paper notes; Europe follows centuries later. Paper is a claim on value held elsewhere — money becomes a ledger entry plus trust.

  5. 1600s–1900s

    Banks & central banks

    Goldsmiths' receipts evolve into banking. The Bank of England (1694) centralizes note issue. Trusted institutions now keep the ledgers — and set the rules.

  6. 1971

    The fiat era

    The United States ends the dollar's convertibility to gold. Since then, every major currency floats on government policy rather than a commodity anchor.

  7. 1950s–2000s

    Digital payments

    Cards, wires, and online banking make money feel digital — but every payment is really an instruction to intermediaries to update their private ledgers.

  8. 2008

    The open question

    Value that moves like email, with no institution in the middle. One obstacle stood in the way for decades: the double-spending problem.

A gold coin

Matter doesn't copy. Scarcity is physics.

A digital coin

Information copies perfectly, for free, forever.

The double-spending problem

If digital cash were just a file, you could pay Alice with it and then pay Bob with an identical copy. Both payments would look perfectly valid, because the copies are perfect. This is the double-spending problem, and for decades it had exactly one solution: appoint a trusted bookkeeper. Your bank doesn't move digital coins around; it maintains a private ledger and promises not to let the same dollar leave your account twice.

That works — but it concentrates enormous power. The bookkeeper decides who may open an account, which payments go through, and how many new units exist. Central banks can and do change the money supply; payment networks can and do freeze transfers. Whether you consider that power well-used or not, it is required by the design. Digital cash pioneers — DigiCash in the 1990s, e-gold, Hashcash, b-money, bit gold — kept circling one unsolved question:

How do you create digital money that cannot be copied — without putting anyone in charge of it?

02 Origins

The birth of Bitcoin

In the autumn of 2008, while the global financial system was being rescued in real time, a nine-page paper appeared on an obscure cryptography mailing list. It solved the double-spending problem with no bookkeeper at all.

Milestones, 2008 → today

  1. Sep 2008

    The crisis

    Lehman Brothers files the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history. Governments launch extraordinary bank rescues, and the world gets a crash course in counterparty risk — the danger that the institution holding your money fails.

  2. Oct 31, 2008

    The whitepaper

    A pseudonymous author, Satoshi Nakamoto, posts “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System” to the metzdowd cryptography mailing list: nine pages proposing online payments “without going through a financial institution.”

  3. Jan 3, 2009

    The genesis block

    Satoshi mines block 0. Embedded in it, forever, is that day's Times of London headline: “Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks” — a timestamp, and perhaps a statement of purpose.

  4. Jan 2009

    First transaction

    Cryptographer Hal Finney downloads the software, tweets “Running bitcoin”, and receives 10 BTC from Satoshi in block 170 — the first bitcoin transaction between two people.

  5. May 22, 2010

    Pizza day

    Laszlo Hanyecz pays 10,000 BTC for two delivered pizzas — about $41 at the time, and the first documented purchase of a physical good with bitcoin. The date is still celebrated (and winced at) annually.

  6. 2010–2011

    Satoshi steps away

    After roughly two years of public work, Satoshi hands the code to other developers, writes “I've moved on to other things”, and vanishes. The network doesn't notice: it was designed not to need its creator.

  7. Nov 28, 2012

    First halving

    At block 210,000 the new-coin reward drops from 50 to 25 BTC — the first beat of a supply schedule fixed in code, repeating every four years. (More in chapter 10.)

  8. Feb 2014

    Mt. Gox collapses

    The largest bitcoin exchange fails, losing roughly 850,000 customer BTC. A painful lesson that still matters: coins held by a company are a promise, not a possession. The protocol itself was untouched.

  9. Aug 2017

    SegWit & a fork

    After a years-long scaling debate, the SegWit upgrade activates. A faction preferring bigger blocks splits off as Bitcoin Cash — a live demonstration that changing Bitcoin's rules requires convincing nearly everyone. (See chapter 9.)

  10. 2018

    Lightning goes live

    The Lightning Network launches on mainnet: a second layer for instant, tiny payments that settle back to the base chain. (Explored in chapter 12.)

  11. 2020–2021

    Halving three, Taproot

    The third halving cuts issuance to 6.25 BTC per block. El Salvador adopts bitcoin as legal tender in 2021, and the Taproot upgrade adds Schnorr signatures — the largest protocol change in years.

  12. 2024

    ETFs & halving four

    U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs are approved in January; traditional brokerage accounts can now hold exposure. In April, the fourth halving cuts the block reward to 3.125 BTC.

  13. Today

    A running experiment

    Sixteen-plus years of uninterrupted operation: tens of thousands of nodes, an industrial mining sector, layered payment networks — and the same 21 million cap it launched with.

Who is Satoshi Nakamoto?

Unknown, by design

Nobody knows. “Satoshi Nakamoto” is the name on the whitepaper and the forum accounts that shepherded Bitcoin through its first two years — a person or group who wrote in fluent English, released the code open-source, and never spent the roughly one million BTC that analyses attribute to their early mining. Every claimed unmasking has fallen apart; every prominent candidate has denied it.

The deeper point is that it doesn't matter. Bitcoin runs on published code and open mathematics that anyone can inspect. Satoshi holds no admin keys, and the system grants its creator no special powers — disappearing may have been the final design decision.

03 Definitions

What is Bitcoin, exactly?

“Bitcoin” names three different things that fit together: a rulebook, a network that enforces the rulebook, and the scarce digital unit the rulebook accounts for.

A protocol

An open-source rulebook: what a valid transaction looks like, what a valid block looks like, how new coins are issued. Published, versioned, and auditable by anyone — like email's rules, but for value.

A network

Tens of thousands of computers — nodes — each holding a full copy of the ledger and independently checking every transaction against the rules. No headquarters, no off switch.

An asset

The unit itself: bitcoin, ticker BTC, divisible into 100 million satoshis. Owning bitcoin means controlling ledger entries — there is no coin file, only the record.

One ledger, thousands of copies

A bank keeps the ledger; Bitcoin keeps a ledger everywhere at once. New entries are only accepted if every copy can verify them by the same rules — which is why no single copy can cheat.

  • Digitally scarce. The issuance schedule caps supply at 21 million; scarcity is enforced by verification, not promises.
  • Decentralized. No company, foundation, or government operates it. Anyone can run a node, mine, or propose code.
  • Permissionless. Sending or receiving requires no account approval — only software and a key pair.
  • Transparent. Every transaction since 2009 is public and independently verifiable by anyone.
  • Fixed policy. Issuance follows code, not committee decisions. Changing it would require the whole network to opt in.
  • Peer-to-peer. Final settlement happens between addresses on the ledger, with no institution required in between.

A note on names: Bitcoin (capital B) usually means the protocol and network; bitcoin (lowercase) the asset. And “blockchain” is Bitcoin's data structure — not a synonym for Bitcoin, and not magic on its own. A blockchain without decentralized consensus is just a slow database.

04 The data structure

The blockchain, hands on

Bitcoin's ledger is a chain of blocks, and the links are made of mathematics. To understand why history is so hard to rewrite, you first need to meet the hash function.

Meet SHA-256

Live — computed in your browser

SHA-256 fingerprint:

A hash function turns any input into a fixed 256-bit fingerprint. Same input, same fingerprint, every time — but change one letter and roughly half of all bits flip. There is no known way to run it backwards, and no shortcut to find an input with a chosen fingerprint. Those three properties power everything below.

Anatomy of a block

Previous hash

The fingerprint of the block before it — the actual “chain” in blockchain.

Merkle root

One hash summarizing every transaction in the block, built by hashing pairs into a tree. Alter any transaction and the root changes.

Timestamp

Roughly when the block was built (consensus tolerates small drift).

Difficulty target

How small the block's own hash must be to count as valid work.

Nonce

A free counter miners twiddle to hunt for a valid hash — the knob of chapter 8.

Transactions

A few thousand of them, on average, headed by the coinbase transaction that pays the miner.

No accounts, only outputs

Bitcoin has no balances table. Coins live as unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs) — like bills in a cash drawer. Alice's 0.8 BTC output is consumed whole; 0.5 goes to Bob and 0.2995 returns to her as change.

Fees are a market

The small difference between inputs and outputs is the fee. Block space is limited, so wallets bid — busier network, higher fees. Miners naturally pick the best-paying waiting transactions.

Bob can be offline

Bob's computer plays no role in receiving. The payment is a ledger update, not a message to him. His wallet simply notices the new output the next time it looks at the chain.

Confirmations deepen

One confirmation means the transaction is in the latest block. Each further block buries it deeper and makes reversal exponentially harder. Six confirmations (≈ 1 hour) is the traditional “settled” benchmark for large sums.

How safe is a random 256-bit secret? There are about 1.2 × 1077 possible private keys — within a few orders of magnitude of the number of atoms in the observable universe. Guessing one is not “hard”; it is statistically indistinguishable from impossible.

Verification needs only the public key — anyone can check, no one can forge. Bitcoin uses ECDSA and, since the 2021 Taproot upgrade, Schnorr signatures.

07 Custody

Wallets hold keys, not coins

The most common misunderstanding in all of Bitcoin: a wallet does not contain bitcoin. Your coins are rows in the global ledger and never leave it. A wallet holds the keys that can sign those rows away — lose the keys, lose the coins.

“My bitcoins are in my wallet.” Intuitive — and wrong. Nothing coin-like is stored on your device.

Inside a real wallet: private keys (usually derived from one seed phrase) plus software that scans the public ledger and drafts signatures. The coins stay on-chain.

The custody spectrum

Most convenientMost secure

Custodial account

An exchange or app holds the keys; you hold a claim. Familiar and recoverable — but it reintroduces the trusted middleman, and history (Mt. Gox and others) shows claims can fail.

Mobile / hot wallet

Keys live on an internet-connected phone or desktop. You control them; malware or phishing can too. Good for spending money, not savings.

Hardware wallet

Keys are generated and kept inside a dedicated offline device that signs transactions without ever exposing them. The standard for self-custodied savings — “cold storage”.

Paper / steel backup

A seed phrase engraved or written and locked away: fully offline, immune to hacking, vulnerable to fire, loss, and anyone who reads it. Usually the backup for a hardware wallet.

Multisignature

Spending requires M of N keys — say, any 2 of 3 kept in different places. One lost or stolen key is survivable. Used by families, companies, and custodians for serious sums. Try it:

08 The engine

Mining: a lottery you enter with electricity

Someone has to add the next page to the ledger. Bitcoin auctions that right by pure chance: find a block whose fingerprint is improbably small, and the network accepts your page — plus a reward. The only way to search is brute force. That is proof of work, and you can do it right now.

Every attempt re-hashes the block header with a new nonce (real double SHA-256, right here in your browser). There is no pattern to exploit — each try is an independent lottery ticket.

    Your browser manages a few hundred thousand hashes per second. The real network performs on the order of 1021 — a sextillion — every second (as of 2026): it gets through a year of your browser's guessing in well under a microsecond. That gap is the security budget.

    Why burn guesses?

    Because guesses are unfakeable. A valid hash proves real computation was spent; there is no shortcut, no forgery, no partial credit. Writing ledger history costs something real — so rewriting it does too.

    The reward

    The winner's block includes a special coinbase transaction paying them newly issued bitcoin — 3.125 BTC per block since the 2024 halving — plus every fee in the block. This is the only way new bitcoin is ever created.

    Winning ≠ ruling

    The lottery earns the right to propose a block, not to decree one. A block containing an invalid transaction is rejected by every node no matter how much work it proves. Mining and validation are separate powers.

    A four-byte knob isn't enough

    The nonce only counts to about 4 billion; industrial miners exhaust it in a blink, then vary the coinbase and timestamp to open fresh search space. The principle is unchanged: enumerate, hash, hope.

    The difficulty thermostat

    Simulation

    More hardware would mean faster blocks — except every 2,016 blocks (≈2 weeks) the network re-tunes the difficulty so blocks keep arriving about every ten minutes no matter how much computing power joins or leaves. Set a hashrate trend and watch the thermostat catch up.

    +20% per period

    The energy question, plainly

    What's measurable

    • Mining consumes industrial-scale electricity — independent estimates in recent years land around the low hundreds of terawatt-hours per year, comparable to a mid-sized country.
    • Miners are ruthlessly cost-driven and cluster on the cheapest power available — increasingly surplus, stranded, or off-peak generation.
    • The energy spent is what makes rewriting history expensive. The cost is the security mechanism, not an accident of inefficiency.

    What's debated

    • Whether securing a monetary network justifies the footprint — a values question that comparisons to banking or gold mining can inform but not settle.
    • How much mining subsidizes renewables, balances grids, or reduces methane flaring versus simply adding demand; studies disagree and the mix varies by region.
    • Hardware turnover and e-waste, and how heavily to weigh them.

    Honest summary: proof of work deliberately converts energy into security. Whether that trade is worth it is a judgment this page leaves to you.

    09 Agreement

    Consensus: one history, no referee

    Thousands of computers that don't know or trust each other end up agreeing, block by block, on a single history. The trick: every node verifies everything itself, and when histories compete, everyone follows the one with the most work behind it.

    Blocks ripple across the globe in seconds; every node checks each one before passing it on.

    What every node checks

    Signatures valid · no output spent twice · no coins minted beyond schedule · block within the size limit · proof-of-work target met. Fail any test and the block is discarded, whoever mined it.

    Forks are routine

    When two miners find valid blocks seconds apart, the network briefly splits. Miners build on whichever they saw first; as soon as the next block lands, one branch carries more work and everyone converges on it. The abandoned block is stale; its transactions return to the queue.

    “Longest” means most work

    Nodes actually follow the chain with the greatest total accumulated proof of work — usually, but not necessarily, the one with the most blocks. A rival chain can't win by being merely longer; it must out-compute the honest network.

    Finality is probabilistic

    No block is ever “officially” final — it just becomes astronomically unlikely to be replaced as blocks pile on top. A handful of confirmations is treated as settled in practice, because reversing them would mean out-working the planet.

    Miners

    Order transactions into blocks and prove work. Powerful, but only within the rules — their blocks are worthless unless nodes accept them.

    vs

    Nodes

    Enforce the rules by refusing anything invalid. Running one takes a home computer — deliberately, so verification stays cheap and authority stays distributed. Rule changes ship only when node operators voluntarily adopt them; 2017's blocksize dispute ended in a split, not a decree, for exactly this reason.

    10 Monetary policy

    Twenty-one million, on a timetable

    Bitcoin's entire monetary policy fits in one sentence: every block mints a fixed reward, and every 210,000 blocks — roughly four years — that reward halves. Run the schedule forward and it sums to just under 21 million coins, ever.

    The halving

    50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6.25 → 3.125 BTC… Each halving (2012, 2016, 2020, 2024, next ≈2028) cuts new issuance in half, on schedule, regardless of demand, politics, or price. The last fraction of a coin arrives around the year 2140.

    Already mostly issued

    About 95% of all bitcoin that will ever exist has been mined already (as of 2026). Annual issuance is now below 1% of supply — lower than gold's typical mine inflation — and it only falls from here.

    Verified, not promised

    Every node continuously enforces the schedule: a block claiming even one satoshi too much is rejected outright. The 21 million cap holds not because anyone promises it, but because breaking it would require nearly every participant to voluntarily adopt new rules.

    Fees take over

    As the subsidy shrinks, transaction fees are designed to become miners' main income. Whether fees alone will fund enough security is one of Bitcoin's genuinely open long-term questions — the experiment runs for another century.

    Two honest footnotes: the true cap is 20,999,999.97 BTC (rounding in the reward formula), and meaningfully less than that will ever circulate — coins in lost keys, including Satoshi's untouched million, are gone for good. Scarcity, if anything, understates itself.

    11 Defense in depth

    Why sixteen years of attacks haven't worked

    Bitcoin secures hundreds of billions in value with no guards, no vault, and no legal department. Its defense is a stack of independent layers — break one and the next is waiting.

    Layer 1

    Cryptography

    SHA-256 and elliptic-curve signatures — the same primitive families that protect global banking and the web. Forging a signature or reversing a hash has no known feasible attack.

    Layer 2

    Independent verification

    Tens of thousands of nodes re-check every rule on every transaction. There is no privileged server to hack — a lie must fool everyone at once.

    Layer 3

    Accumulated work

    History is buried under a mountain of energy. Rewriting even yesterday means redoing the whole world's mining while it keeps extending the real chain.

    Layer 4

    Economic incentives

    The hardware and power needed to attack earn far more by mining honestly — and a successful attack would devalue the very prize it targets. Rationality is a security feature.

    Race the honest chain

    Acquiring most of the world's fastest specialized hardware plus gigawatts of power, to briefly disrupt a network whose value your attack undermines: possible in theory, self-defeating in practice. Smaller proof-of-work networks have suffered 51% attacks — Bitcoin's scale is the moat.

    The worst day: August 15, 2010

    Case study

    A bug in early code let someone conjure 184 billion BTC out of an integer overflow — in one transaction. Within hours, developers shipped a fix, and node operators adopted it; the network reorganized onto a corrected chain, erasing the exploit. Nothing like it has happened since — but it's the honest reminder that Bitcoin's rules are enforced by people choosing to run software, and its security record is a practice, not a law of nature.

    12 Layer 2

    Lightning: settle later, pay now

    Global consensus is Bitcoin's superpower and its bottleneck: every node checks every transaction, so the base chain handles only a handful per second, and a coffee shouldn't wait ten minutes. The Lightning Network moves everyday payments off the chain — without giving up its guarantees.

    Alice and Bob each hold 0.05 BTC. Open a channel to begin.

    Payment channels

    Two parties lock funds in a shared on-chain address, then exchange signed balance updates directly — thousands of them, instantly, for fractions of a cent. Only the open and the close ever touch the blockchain.

    Routing

    No channel to the coffee shop? No problem: payments hop across connected channels — Alice → Bob → Carol — and cryptographic locks (HTLCs) guarantee every hop settles or none does. Nobody along the path can steal the payment.

    Cheating loses money

    Try to close a channel with an old, more favorable balance and the protocol lets your counterparty claim everything in it during a dispute window. Honesty is enforced by penalty, not politeness.

    The layered future

    The base chain becomes the settlement court — slow, global, final; Lightning becomes the checkout lane — instant, cheap, private-ish. Tradeoffs remain: funds must be locked in advance, and recipients need to be reachable. Layer 2 is engineering, not magic.

    13 Straight answers

    Common misconceptions, answered carefully

    Sixteen years in, most Bitcoin arguments still orbit the same six questions. Each deserves a real answer — not a slogan from either side.

    Bitcoin is pseudonymous: addresses aren't names, but every transaction is public forever, and modern chain analysis is very good at clustering addresses and linking them to identities — especially once coins touch a regulated exchange. Law enforcement has repeatedly traced bitcoin years after the fact. Privacy techniques exist, but out of the box Bitcoin is more transparent than a bank account, not less.

    The headlines are real, but they describe businesses around Bitcoin — exchanges breached, passwords phished, keys stolen — the digital equivalent of bank robberies, not broken currency. The protocol itself — its cryptography and consensus — has never been successfully attacked in sixteen years while guarding an ever-larger bounty. Distinguish the vault from the valet.

    True: no commodity or government stands behind bitcoin. But “backing” deserves scrutiny — gold is backed by nothing either; it has properties (scarcity, durability) and believers. Fiat currency is backed by state authority and tax demand. Bitcoin's case rests on enforced scarcity, an unbreakable settlement network, and the network effect of everyone who accepts it. Whether those justify any particular value is a market judgment — what's objective is that the scarcity, unlike most, is independently verifiable by anyone.

    Bitcoin measurably consumes country-scale electricity — that part isn't in dispute. “Waste” is the contested word: it implies the output (a neutral, borderless settlement system) isn't worth the input, which is a values judgment, not a measurement. Compare honestly — banking infrastructure and gold mining also consume enormously — and note the real nuances: miners chase cheap, often surplus power, yet demand is demand. Reasonable people land differently; the numbers themselves are in chapter 8.

    Governments regulate Bitcoin's edges effectively — exchanges, taxes, businesses — and several have banned it outright. But switching the network off means silencing every node in every jurisdiction at once; when China expelled all bitcoin mining in 2021 — then roughly half the network — blocks kept arriving, and the hashrate relocated and recovered within months. Policy shapes adoption and price; the protocol itself has proven very hard to reach.

    The code has been copied thousands of times — that's what altcoins and forks like Bitcoin Cash are. What a copy cannot take is the network: the miners securing it, the nodes enforcing it, the users accepting it, and sixteen years of unbroken history. Scarcity lives per-network, and a new network starts from zero on all of it. Copying Bitcoin's code and expecting Bitcoin's properties is copying a map and expecting the territory.
