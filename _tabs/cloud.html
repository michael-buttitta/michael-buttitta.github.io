01 First principles

What is “the cloud”?

The cloud is not a mysterious place in the sky. It is a network of real, physical computers — millions of them — racked in professionally managed buildings called data centers, rented out over the internet. Every “cloud” photo you upload and every “cloud” app you open lands on a hard drive or a CPU in one of these buildings.

Follow a single request below. When you open a web app, your laptop doesn’t talk to a cloud — it talks through your router, your internet provider, and the internet backbone until it reaches a specific building, a specific rack, and a specific machine. The fluffy cloud icon on architecture diagrams is just shorthand for “somebody else’s well-run data center.”

What makes a data center different from a very large closet full of computers is everything wrapped around the machines. A modern facility is an exercise in engineering paranoia — every system that could fail has a backup, and usually the backup has a backup:

Power

Dual utility feeds, room-sized battery banks (UPS) that carry the load instantly when the grid blips, and diesel generators that can run for days. A large data center draws as much power as a small town.

Cooling

Servers turn nearly every watt into heat. Facilities alternate “cold aisles” and “hot aisles,” push chilled air through the racks, and increasingly pipe liquid directly to the hottest chips. Cooling is often the second-largest cost after the hardware itself.

Networking

Multiple independent fiber routes into the building, massive internal switch fabrics moving terabits per second between racks, and direct connections to internet exchanges — so no single cut cable takes the facility offline.

Redundancy

Everything is provisioned N+1 or better: one more generator, chiller, and network path than the building strictly needs. Individual servers are expected to die — the design goal is that nobody outside the building ever notices.

So the honest one-line definition: cloud computing is renting slices of other people’s industrial-grade computers over the internet, on demand, by the second. The rest of this page is about how that trick actually works — and why it reshaped the software industry.

02 Origins

Why cloud computing exists

Before the cloud, launching software meant buying computers. You estimated your peak traffic, ordered servers months in advance, installed them in a rented cage, and prayed your estimate was right. Guess low and your site collapsed on its best day. Guess high and you paid for machines that sat idle at 3 a.m. — every night, forever.

1

2

Maintains hardware — staff to rack, cable, patch, and replace failed disks.

3

4

Overprovisions for the worst case — capacity sized for the biggest day of the year.

5

Repeats every time the business grows — or shrinks.

In the mid-2000s, companies running enormous websites realized they had accidentally become world-class at exactly this problem — and that the machinery they’d built to run their own services could be rented out. Instead of buying servers, a customer could click a button and have a machine in sixty seconds, billed by the hour, returned just as easily. Computing became operating expense instead of capital expense, and capacity became elastic.

The classic analogy is electricity: you don’t run a generator behind your house; you plug into a grid and pay for what you use. The analogy is genuinely useful — shared infrastructure, metered billing, professional operations — but imperfect. Electrons are interchangeable; computing isn’t. Your data has gravity, your code has vendor-specific dependencies, and moving providers is much harder than switching power companies. Keep the analogy, keep its limits.

03 The landscape

Meet the major cloud providers

Three companies operate most of the world’s public cloud: Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud Platform. They are different ecosystems solving the same problems — the concepts you learn on this page transfer between all of them.

Amazon Web Services

History. Launched in 2006 with S3 (storage) and EC2 (rentable servers), born from Amazon’s experience running its own retail infrastructure. First mover, and still the largest provider by market share.

Typical strengths. The broadest service catalog (hundreds of services), the most mature ecosystem of tools and third-party integrations, and the largest hiring pool of experienced engineers.

Common customers. Startups that grew up on it (Netflix, Airbnb, Stripe are famously heavy users), plus enterprises of every size and large public-sector deployments.

Global footprint. Dozens of regions on six continents, each with multiple availability zones.

Example services. EC2 (virtual machines), S3 (object storage), Lambda (serverless functions), RDS (managed databases), EKS (managed Kubernetes), CloudFront (CDN).

Microsoft Azure

History. Announced in 2008 and launched in 2010, built on Microsoft’s decades of enterprise software and its own global services (Office, Xbox, Bing).

Typical strengths. Deep integration with the Microsoft stack — Windows Server, Active Directory, Office 365, .NET — and strong hybrid-cloud tooling for companies that keep some infrastructure on-premises.

Common customers. Large enterprises, organizations already licensed on Microsoft software, and regulated industries with hybrid requirements.

Global footprint. More announced regions than any other provider, with particular depth in enterprise and government markets.

Example services. Azure Virtual Machines, Blob Storage, Azure Functions, Azure SQL Database, AKS (managed Kubernetes), Azure Front Door / CDN.

Google Cloud Platform

History. Grew from App Engine (2008) into a full platform, exposing the infrastructure Google built to run Search, Gmail, and YouTube — including technologies like Borg, the internal system that inspired Kubernetes.

Typical strengths. Data analytics and machine learning services (BigQuery is a flagship), a global private network, and first-party Kubernetes expertise — Google created and open-sourced it.

Common customers. Data-heavy companies, ML/AI teams, and organizations standardized on Kubernetes and open-source tooling.

Global footprint. Dozens of regions linked by one of the largest private fiber networks in the world.

Example services. Compute Engine (virtual machines), Cloud Storage, Cloud Functions / Cloud Run, Cloud SQL, GKE (managed Kubernetes), Cloud CDN.

The names differ; the shapes rhyme. Whatever provider you meet, look for the same five primitives — rentable machines, object storage, functions, managed databases, managed Kubernetes — and you can orient yourself in minutes:

04 Geography

Data centers, regions & availability zones

The cloud is not one place — it’s a planet-wide mesh of campuses. Providers organize the world into regions (a metro area with a cluster of data centers) and each region into availability zones (independent facilities with separate power, cooling, and networking). This geography is what makes applications fast and hard to kill.

Physics sets the rules: signals in fiber travel at roughly two-thirds the speed of light, so every 1,000 km adds about 10 ms of round trip. That’s why providers put regions near people. Click anywhere on the map — or pick a city — and watch the request route to the nearest region.

Zoom in on any one region and there’s a deliberate hierarchy of failure domains. Each level exists so that a failure at that level stays contained:

Region

A metro area (“Ireland”, “Oregon”) with 2–6 availability zones. Your data usually stays inside the region you choose — which is also how providers help you meet data-residency laws.

Availability zone

One or more data centers with independent power, cooling, and network, far enough apart that a fire or flood in one shouldn’t touch another — but close enough for millisecond links. Serious systems run in at least two.

Rack

A cabinet of a few dozen servers sharing a top-of-rack switch and power feed. Schedulers deliberately spread your machines across racks so one failed switch doesn’t take out all your replicas.

Server

The actual computer — the same CPUs, memory, and SSDs explored in the CPU and memory exhibits, just dense, headless, and expected to be replaceable.

This hierarchy is the backbone of high availability (run copies in multiple zones; a zone outage becomes a non-event) and disaster recovery (replicate to a second region; even a regional catastrophe — earthquake, grid failure — leaves you a place to fail over to). Geography, turned into an engineering tool.

05 The big idea

Virtual machines: one computer, many computers

The technology that made cloud computing possible is virtualization: a layer of software called a hypervisor that slices one physical server into many isolated virtual machines, each believing it owns the whole computer.

A physical server in a cloud data center might have 64 CPU cores and hundreds of gigabytes of RAM — far more than most customers need. The hypervisor sits directly on the hardware and hands out slices: two cores and 8 GB to this customer’s VM, eight cores and 32 GB to that one. Each VM boots its own operating system and cannot see, read, or crash its neighbors. That isolation is what makes multi-tenancy — strangers safely sharing one machine — possible, and multi-tenancy is what makes renting by the hour economical.

    Under the hood the hypervisor is a scheduler, much like the operating system schedulers described in the CPU exhibit: it time-slices physical cores among virtual ones, uses hardware virtualization features built into modern processors to keep guests isolated at silicon speed, and maps each VM’s “physical” memory onto real DRAM. When you click “launch instance” on any cloud console, this is what happens: a scheduler finds a host with free capacity, the hypervisor carves out your slice, and a machine that didn’t exist forty seconds ago accepts your login.

    Why this revolutionized everything: before virtualization, one server ran one workload, and the average machine idled below 15% utilization. Virtualization let providers pack many tenants per box, driving utilization — and driving down the price of renting a computer — by an order of magnitude. Every chapter after this one builds on that trick.

    06 Shipping code

    Containers & Kubernetes

    Virtual machines solved “share the hardware.” Containers solved a different problem: “it works on my machine.” A container packages an application with every library it needs into one sealed, portable unit that runs identically on a laptop, a server, or any cloud.

    The key difference from a VM: a container doesn’t bring its own operating system. All containers on a machine share the host’s OS kernel, isolated from each other by kernel features rather than by a hypervisor. That makes them megabytes instead of gigabytes, and startable in milliseconds instead of minutes — light enough to run dozens per machine and to treat them as disposable.

    Once you have hundreds of disposable containers, a new question appears: who decides where each one runs, restarts the ones that crash, and adds more when traffic grows? That job is orchestration, and the industry-standard orchestrator is Kubernetes. You declare a desired state — “keep 6 replicas of this service running” — and its scheduler continuously works to make reality match.     07 Plumbing

    Networking in the cloud

    Every cloud application lives inside a virtual network — a software-defined private network you design: its address ranges, its subnets, its firewall rules. The physical switches and fiber belong to the provider; the topology is yours.

    The near-universal pattern is to split that network into a public subnet — reachable from the internet, holding only the front door — and private subnets where application servers and databases live, unreachable from outside. Between every hop stand security groups: per-server firewalls that only pass traffic you explicitly allow. Send a request through the stack below and watch each checkpoint.

    DNS

    The internet’s phone book: turns app.example.com into an IP address. Cloud DNS services also steer users to the nearest healthy region.

    Load balancer

    The front door. Accepts all incoming traffic, spreads it across healthy servers, and quietly stops sending to any that fail its health checks.

    Security groups

    Firewalls attached to each server: “the database accepts port 5432, only from the app tier, and nothing else.” Deny is the default.

    Public vs private subnets

    Public subnets can route to the internet; private ones can’t be reached from it. The blast radius of a mistake shrinks with every layer an attacker can’t touch.

    08 Where data lives

    Storage: four shapes of data

    “Store my data in the cloud” hides a real decision. Cloud platforms offer several distinct kinds of storage, each with its own physics, price, and sweet spot — an extension of the memory hierarchy beyond a single machine.

    Object storage

    S3 · Azure Blob · Cloud Storage

    A bottomless bucket of files addressed by name over HTTP. No folders, no file locking — just put, get, delete. Typically replicated across three availability zones, giving famous “eleven nines” durability claims. Cheap, effectively infinite, a few milliseconds per access.

    Best for: images, video, backups, logs, data lakes, static websites.

    Block storage

    EBS · Azure Managed Disks · Persistent Disk

    A virtual hard drive attached to one VM — the cloud version of the SSD in your laptop. Fast, low-latency, survives the VM being stopped or replaced. What your operating system and database actually write to.

    Best for: boot disks, database volumes, anything needing a real filesystem.

    File storage

    EFS · Azure Files · Filestore

    A shared network drive many machines mount at once, with familiar directories and permissions. Slower than block storage, but shared state without custom code.

    Best for: shared assets across a fleet, lift-and-shift apps that expect a filesystem, home directories.

    Databases

    RDS · Azure SQL · Cloud SQL · DynamoDB · BigQuery

    Not raw storage but a managed service on top of it: queries, indexes, transactions, automated backups, and replication to a standby in another zone. Relational for structured records; key-value and document stores for internet-scale lookup tables; warehouses for analytics.

    Best for: anything you query — users, orders, inventory, events.

    One principle explains most cloud durability numbers: replication. Object stores and managed databases don’t keep your data on one very good disk — they keep it on several ordinary disks in several buildings, and repair copies automatically when hardware dies. Durability is a property of the system, not the device.

    09 Elasticity

    Auto scaling & load balancing

    This is the cloud’s signature move: capacity that follows demand. A load balancer spreads traffic across a fleet of identical servers; an auto scaling policy watches utilization and changes the size of the fleet — no human in the loop.

    Drag the slider from a quiet hundred users to a viral million and watch the system respond: instances boot when utilization climbs, drain and terminate when it falls. Each on-screen server may represent many real instances at the high end — the counter tells the truth.

    Illustrative numbers: each instance handles ~2,000 concurrent users at a target 70% utilization, at $0.10 per instance-hour. Real policies also react to request latency, queue depth, or custom metrics.

    Two details make this work in practice. Health checks: the load balancer constantly probes every instance and silently stops routing to any that fail — which is also how zero-downtime deployments work (new instances join, old ones drain, users never notice). And statelessness: instances can only be disposable if no irreplaceable data lives on them — state belongs in the databases and object stores from the previous chapter. Elasticity isn’t one feature; it’s an architecture.

    10 No servers?

    Serverless computing

    Auto scaling still leaves you managing a fleet. Serverless asks: what if the fleet size were zero until the moment a request arrives? You upload a function — just code — and the platform runs it on demand, scales it to thousands of parallel copies, and bills you only for the milliseconds it actually runs.

    Servers still exist, of course — you just never see them. Fire single requests and bursts at the function below and watch execution environments appear, stay warm briefly for reuse, and vanish. The first request to hit a cold environment pays a cold start — the platform has to set up the runtime before your code runs.

    Event-driven

    Functions are triggered by events: an HTTP request, a file landing in object storage, a message on a queue, a timer. Glue code for the cloud.

    Stateless

    Each invocation starts fresh — anything worth keeping goes to a database or object store. That constraint is exactly what lets the platform scale copies freely.

    Pay-per-use

    An API that gets 10 requests a day costs effectively nothing. The trade: cold-start latency, execution time limits, and less control over the runtime.

    Versus VMs

    A VM is always on, always billed, fully yours to configure. Functions fit spiky and event-shaped work; steady heavy traffic often still favors VMs or containers.

    11 Defense

    Cloud security & the shared responsibility model

    Cloud providers run some of the most attacked — and most defended — infrastructure on Earth. But “the cloud is secure” does not mean your app is secure. Security is explicitly split: the provider secures the cloud itself; you secure what you build in it.

    Where exactly the line sits depends on how much of the stack you rent. Switch between service models below — the boundary moves, but it never disappears. Misconfigured storage buckets and leaked credentials, not hypervisor exploits, cause the overwhelming majority of real cloud breaches — and those are all on the customer’s side of the line.

    2. Data & its classification

    On the customer’s side of the line, a handful of practices do most of the work:

    Identity & Access Management

    Every person and program gets an identity, and every action is checked against policy. The golden rule is least privilege: grant the minimum access that works, nothing more.

    Multi-factor authentication

    A password alone shouldn’t open anything important. MFA turns a stolen credential from a catastrophe into an inconvenience.

    Encryption in transit

    TLS/HTTPS on every connection — the handshakes, certificates, and ciphers dissected in the cryptography exhibit — so traffic can’t be read or altered en route.

    Encryption at rest

    Disks, object stores, and database volumes encrypted with managed keys, so a stolen drive is just noise. Providers make this a checkbox; the customer’s job is to leave it checked.

    Zero trust

    Never assume the internal network is safe. Authenticate every request, every service, every time — “inside the firewall” stops being a security boundary.

    Backups, logging & monitoring

    Immutable backups tested by actually restoring them; audit logs of who did what; alerts on the unexpected. Boring, decisive, and the difference between an incident and a headline.

    13 In the wild

    Real-world use cases

    The same building blocks compose into radically different systems. These sketches describe common architectural patterns for each kind of workload — not any company’s confidential internals.

    Small business website

    Likely ingredients: static site in object storage behind a CDN, a managed database, maybe one small VM or a few functions for forms.

    Why cloud helps: near-zero cost at low traffic, no hardware to maintain, and the site survives a mention on national TV without changes.

    Scale needs: tiny — which is exactly the point: the same platform serves both ends of this list.

    Netflix-style video streaming

    Likely ingredients: massive object storage for video masters, huge CDN capacity for delivery, fleets of encoding workers, data pipelines for recommendations.

    Why cloud helps: encoding is spiky (new releases), viewing is global, and per-country capacity planning would be brutal. Netflix famously runs on a public cloud plus its own purpose-built CDN appliances inside ISPs.

    Scale needs: a large share of global downstream internet traffic at peak.

    Spotify-style music service

    Likely ingredients: object storage for audio, CDN for delivery, streaming data pipelines for play events, ML training for personalized playlists.

    Why cloud helps: the interesting problems are data problems — managed analytics and ML infrastructure lets engineers work on recommendations, not rack maintenance.

    Scale needs: hundreds of millions of users, tens of billions of daily events.

    Uber-style ride hailing

    Likely ingredients: real-time location streams, geospatial matching services, event queues, regional deployments for latency, surge pricing analytics.

    Why cloud helps: demand is extremely spiky (rain, concerts, New Year’s Eve) and city-by-city expansion means capacity needs appear overnight in new geographies.

    Scale needs: millions of concurrent location updates with tight latency budgets.

    Airbnb-style marketplace

    Likely ingredients: search and ranking services, object storage + CDN for listing photos, relational databases for bookings and payments, fraud-detection ML.

    Why cloud helps: bookings are transactional and must not be lost; photo storage grows without bound; traffic peaks seasonally.

    Scale needs: global reads, strongly consistent writes for the money paths.

    Online banking

    Likely ingredients: often hybrid: core ledgers on-premises or in a private cloud for regulatory reasons, customer-facing apps and analytics in a public cloud, heavy IAM, encryption, and audit logging everywhere.

    Why cloud helps: mobile banking traffic scales like any consumer app, while compliance workloads (fraud scoring, reporting) burst well on elastic compute.

    Scale needs: moderate traffic, extreme correctness, availability, and auditability.

    AI platforms

    Likely ingredients: clusters of GPU-equipped instances — the accelerators from the GPU exhibit — fast object storage for training data, high-bandwidth interconnects, and inference fleets that auto scale with usage.

    Why cloud helps: GPU supercomputers are among the most capital-intensive machines ever built; renting them turned model training from a national-lab project into a startup activity.

    Scale needs: thousands of accelerators for training runs; latency-sensitive inference at global scale.

    E-commerce

    Likely ingredients: the full canonical stack from chapter 12 — CDN, auto scaling web tier, cache, relational database — plus search, payments, and inventory services.

    Why cloud helps: Black Friday. Traffic can be 10–50× baseline for a few hours a year; elasticity is the difference between record sales and a very public outage.

    Scale needs: extreme peaks, strict cart/checkout correctness.

    Healthcare systems

    Likely ingredients: compliant storage for patient records (encryption, access logging, regional data residency), imaging archives in object storage, ML for diagnostics support.

    Why cloud helps: providers offer certified compliance baselines (HIPAA-eligible services and equivalents) that small clinics could never build alone — though configuration responsibility stays with the customer.

    Scale needs: modest traffic, very high privacy and durability bars.

    Online gaming

    Likely ingredients: regional fleets of game servers close to players for low latency, matchmaking services, global state in fast key-value stores, spiky launch-day scaling.

    Why cloud helps: a hit game’s launch is the most hostile traffic pattern in software — millions of players in hours, then a long decay. Elastic regional capacity is the only sane answer.

    Scale needs: single-digit-millisecond latency budgets, brutal launch spikes.

    Blockchain infrastructure

    Likely ingredients: full nodes, indexers, and RPC API services for networks like Bitcoin and other blockchains often run on cloud VMs; mining pool coordinators and analytics pipelines too.

    Why cloud helps: nodes are ordinary always-on workloads with growing disks — classic cloud territory.

    The tension: these networks exist to be decentralized — if most nodes ran in one provider’s cloud, that provider would become the very central point of failure the design tries to avoid. Many operators deliberately run nodes on independent hardware for exactly that reason.

    Scientific computing

    Likely ingredients: burst clusters of thousands of spot-priced VMs for simulations, object storage data lakes, workflow orchestrators.

    Why cloud helps: a research team can rent a top-500-class cluster for an afternoon, answer a question, and return it — a purchase impossible to justify for occasional use.

    Scale needs: enormous but brief.

    14 Reality check

    Common misconceptions

    Cloud discourse is full of slogans. Each one below holds a grain of truth — click to see where it breaks down.

    Partly true Physically, yes — and it’s healthy to remember that. But the phrase hides what you’re actually buying: global failover, eleven-nines storage, managed databases, and sixty-second provisioning. Nobody’s spare computer does that.

    False Elastic and spiky workloads: usually yes. Large, steady, predictable workloads: often no — several companies have famously saved millions by moving stable fleets back to owned hardware. The honest answer is “cheaper per unit of flexibility, not per CPU-hour.”

    False The provider secures the infrastructure — and does it extraordinarily well. Your bucket permissions, IAM policies, and leaked API keys are yours, and they cause the vast majority of real cloud breaches. See the shared responsibility model above.

    False Data-residency law, latency to a factory floor, existing hardware investments, or plain economics can all argue for keeping some workloads on-premises. That’s why hybrid cloud (mixing both) is a mainstream architecture, not a compromise.

    It depends Using several providers can reduce lock-in and satisfy regulators — at the price of doubled expertise, lowest-common-denominator services, and complex networking. For many teams, depth on one platform beats shallow presence on three.

    False They stack. In public clouds your containers almost always run inside VMs — the hypervisor provides the hard multi-tenant boundary; containers provide packaging and density on top of it.

    Obviously not Your code runs on very real servers — provisioned, patched, and scaled by the provider in the seconds around each invocation. “Serverless” describes your experience (no servers to manage), not the physics.

    False “Public” describes who can rent the infrastructure (anyone), not who can read your data. Tenants are isolated by the virtualization and encryption covered above. A private cloud is the same technology operated for a single organization.
