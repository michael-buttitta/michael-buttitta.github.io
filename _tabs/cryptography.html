01 The problem

Why cryptography exists

Imagine mailing a private letter on a postcard. Every mail carrier, sorter, and nosy neighbor along the route can read it — or quietly rewrite it. Now imagine sending a password, a bank transfer, or a medical record across the internet, where every message is copied through dozens of machines you have never met. How do you stop strangers from reading it, changing it, or pretending to be you?

Watch a message travel from Alice to Bob below. It hops through relays — routers, Wi-Fi access points, servers — and one of them, Eve, is eavesdropping. Send the message in the clear, then send it encrypted, and compare what Eve actually sees.

Cryptography is the mathematics of communicating in the presence of adversaries. Everything on this page — and essentially all of internet security — is built to deliver four guarantees:

Confidentiality

Only the intended recipient can read the message. Everyone else sees noise. This is what encryption provides.

Integrity

The message that arrives is exactly the message that was sent — any tampering, even one flipped bit, is detected. This is what hashes and authentication codes provide.

Authentication

You are really talking to who you think you are — your bank, not an impostor with a convincing login page. This is what certificates and signatures provide.

Non-repudiation

The sender cannot later deny sending the message — a digital signature is proof that only the holder of a specific private key could have produced it.

The rest of this page builds these guarantees up one primitive at a time — ciphers, keys, hashes, signatures, certificates — until the whole machinery of HTTPS clicks into place.

02 Origins

A brief history of secrets

For most of its 2,000-year history, cryptography was a military craft: generals hiding orders from enemies. In a single generation it became the invisible infrastructure of everyday life. Here is how.

  1. The Caesar cipher

    Julius Caesar shifted every letter of his military messages three places down the alphabet. Trivial today — there are only 25 possible shifts to try — but effective against illiterate couriers. Try it yourself below.

  2. Substitution ciphers & frequency analysis

    Scrambling the whole alphabet gives 400 septillion possible keys — yet Arab scholar al-Kindi broke it anyway by counting letter frequencies. The lesson still holds: a huge keyspace means nothing if the cipher leaks patterns.

  3. Enigma & wartime codebreaking

    Nazi Germany’s Enigma machine changed its substitution with every keystroke — about 158 quintillion settings. Polish and British cryptanalysts, including Alan Turing at Bletchley Park, broke it with early computing machinery, shortening the war and midwifing computer science itself.

  4. DES — a public standard

    The US standardized the Data Encryption Standard: for the first time, a cipher anyone could study, implement, and attack in the open. Its 56-bit key eventually fell to brute force, teaching the field to design for decades of hardware growth.

  5. Diffie–Hellman & RSA — the public-key revolution

    Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman asked a heretical question: could two strangers agree on a secret in public? Their key exchange, followed by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman’s RSA cryptosystem, split keys into public and private halves — the idea that makes internet-scale security possible.

  6. AES — the workhorse

    After a five-year open worldwide competition, the Rijndael cipher became the Advanced Encryption Standard. It now encrypts your Wi-Fi, your disk, your messages — often via dedicated CPU instructions (see the CPU exhibit for how hardware accelerates it).

  7. Elliptic-curve cryptography

    A different kind of math — points on curves instead of giant primes — delivers RSA-level security with far smaller keys: a 256-bit ECC key roughly matches a 3,072-bit RSA key. Smaller, faster keys made strong crypto practical on phones and smart cards.

  8. SSL → TLS — securing the web

    Netscape’s SSL, refined into TLS, packaged key exchange, certificates, and symmetric encryption into one handshake every browser performs billions of times a day. The padlock was born.

  9. Cryptography everywhere

    Over 95% of web traffic is encrypted. Your phone signs its software updates, your messages are end-to-end encrypted, and entire monetary systems (see Understanding Bitcoin) are built from nothing but hashes and signatures. Military secrecy became civilian infrastructure.

03 One shared secret

Symmetric encryption

The oldest idea in cryptography, perfected: one secret key both locks and unlocks the message. Modern symmetric ciphers like AES are so fast and so strong that brute-forcing a single 256-bit key would outlast the universe — the catch is that both sides must already share the key.

Message→ Secret key→ Encrypt→ Ciphertext→ Same secret key→ Decrypt→ Message

One shared secret. The same key encrypts and decrypts — like a physical lock where one key both locks and unlocks.
Fast. AES encrypts gigabytes per second; modern CPUs execute it with dedicated AES-NI instructions in hardware.
Efficient. Ciphertext is barely larger than the message. This is why bulk data — disks, video calls, this web page — always uses symmetric encryption.
The key-distribution problem. How do Alice and Bob agree on the key in the first place, if every channel between them is being watched? For centuries the only answer was couriers and locked briefcases. The next two chapters are the modern answer.

04 The revolution

Asymmetric encryption

In 1976 cryptographers found a way to split the key in two: a public key you can shout from the rooftops, and a private key you never reveal. What one half locks, only the other half unlocks. Strangers can now exchange secrets without ever meeting.

The intuition: Bob’s public key is an open padlock he hands out freely. Anyone can snap it shut on a box — but once closed, only Bob’s private key opens it. Alice never needs Bob’s secret; she only needs his padlock. Watch it happen:

Public keys can be shared because encrypting is a one-way street: turning the public key back into the private key would require factoring enormous numbers (RSA) or reversing elliptic-curve math (ECC) — problems with no known efficient solution.
Private keys must stay secret — whoever holds the private key is you, mathematically. Where keys physically live (and why memory protection matters) is a story the Memory & Storage exhibit tells.
The trade-off: asymmetric operations are thousands of times slower than AES and can only handle small payloads. So real systems use asymmetric crypto once — to agree on a symmetric key — then let AES do the heavy lifting. That hybrid is exactly what TLS does, as you’ll see in chapter 07.

05 Digital fingerprints

Hash functions

A cryptographic hash function reads any input — a word, a novel, a 4 GB movie — and produces a fixed-size fingerprint. Change one bit of the input and the fingerprint changes beyond recognition. It is the internet’s tamper-evident seal.

SHA-256
Bits changed vs previous input–

A good hash flips about half of all output bits on any change — the avalanche effect. There is no pattern to exploit, no way to work backwards.

Deterministic. The same input always produces the same digest — on any machine, forever. That is what makes fingerprints comparable.
One-way. Going from digest back to input is computationally infeasible. A hash is a fingerprint, not a container: the original is not “inside” it.
Collision-resistant. Finding two different inputs with the same SHA-256 digest would take on the order of 2128 attempts — billions of times the age of the universe at any conceivable speed.
Integrity everywhere. Software downloads publish their hash so you can verify nothing was corrupted or backdoored. Blockchains chain block hashes together so history itself becomes tamper-evident, and Bitcoin’s mining is a global race to find lucky hashes.

06 Proof of authorship

Digital signatures

Encryption proves secrecy. Signatures prove authenticity. A digital signature runs asymmetric keys in reverse: you sign with your private key, and anyone can verify with your public key — proving the message came from you and hasn’t changed since.

Document→ Hash→ Private key signs→ Signature→ Public key verifies→ Valid / forged

What is actually signed is the hash of the document — which is why signatures are tiny and fast even for huge files, and why any edit breaks them.
Authentication + integrity + non-repudiation in one primitive: only the private key could have made it, the content is pinned, and the signer cannot deny it later.
You use these constantly. Every app update, every HTTPS certificate, every Bitcoin transaction is a digital signature being verified on your behalf.

07 The padlock

Certificates & HTTPS

Encryption alone has a blind spot: you might be having a perfectly secure conversation with the wrong person. Certificates solve identity; TLS assembles everything on this page into the handshake behind the padlock in your address bar.

A certificate is a signed statement: “this public key belongs to yourbank.com” — signed by a Certificate Authority (CA), an organization your browser already trusts and whose public keys ship inside it. The CA verified the site’s identity before signing, so an impostor can’t present a certificate for a domain they don’t control. Step through a TLS handshake:

  1. Hello. Your browser opens the connection: “here are the cipher suites I speak, and a fresh random number.”
  2. Certificate. The server replies with its certificate — its public key plus the CA’s signature vouching for its identity.
  3. Verification. The browser checks the CA’s signature against its built-in trust store, the domain name, and the expiry date. Any mismatch → the full-page warning you’ve seen.
  4. Key exchange. Browser and server run an ephemeral Diffie–Hellman exchange (chapter 09) to derive a session key no eavesdropper can compute.
  5. Encrypted. Both switch to symmetric AES with the session key. The padlock closes — the whole ceremony took a few round-trips.

Why HTTPS prevents impersonation: an attacker can intercept traffic, but they cannot produce a certificate for yourbank.com that a trusted CA signed — and without the site’s private key, they cannot complete the handshake as that site.
Chain of trust. Certificates form a chain: site → intermediate CA → root CA. Your OS and browser ship with ~100 audited root CAs; every padlock traces back to one of them.
The padlock means the connection is secure, not the site. A phishing site can have flawless HTTPS. The padlock says “nobody is in the middle” — it does not say “these people are honest.” (More in chapter 11.)

08 Storing secrets

Password security

A well-run website cannot tell you your own password — because it never stored it. It stored a salted hash. When breaches happen (and they do), this single design decision separates a bad week from a catastrophe.

Password→ + random salt→ Slow hash→ Digest→ Database

Hashing, not encryption. The site never needs your password back — at login it hashes what you typed and compares digests. One-way is exactly the property you want here.
Salting defeats rainbow tables. Attackers once precomputed hashes of millions of common passwords (“rainbow tables”) and looked breached digests up instantly. A unique random salt per user makes every hash unique — the precomputed table is worthless, and identical passwords no longer produce identical hashes.
Slow on purpose. Real systems use deliberately expensive hashes (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2) so that even with stolen hashes, guessing billions of passwords per second is impossible. (The demo above uses fast SHA-256 for illustration only.)
None of this saves a weak password. Hashing protects the database, not a password an attacker can guess in a hundred tries. Long, unique passwords — ideally from a password manager (itself an encrypted vault, chapter 10) — remain your half of the bargain.

09 Agreeing in public

Key exchange

The most counterintuitive trick in cryptography: two strangers shout at each other across a crowded room — and end up sharing a secret nobody else in the room knows. This is Diffie–Hellman key exchange, and it runs every time you open an HTTPS page.

The classic intuition uses paint. Mixing colors is easy; un-mixing them is practically impossible — a one-way operation, like the math underneath (modular exponentiation or elliptic-curve multiplication). Watch Alice and Bob mix their way to a shared secret while Eve captures everything that crosses the wire:

Session keys. The shared secret becomes a temporary symmetric key for exactly one conversation, then is thrown away. Fresh session, fresh key.
Forward secrecy. Because session keys are ephemeral and never sent anywhere, even a future theft of the server’s long-term private key cannot decrypt recorded past traffic. Yesterday’s conversations stay sealed forever.
Exchange ≠ identity. Diffie–Hellman guarantees a private channel but not with whom — that is why TLS pairs it with certificates (chapter 07): signatures prove identity, the exchange builds the secret.

11 Setting the record straight

Common misconceptions

Cryptography attracts confident half-truths. Each card states a common belief — open it for what’s actually going on.

Myth “Hashing is just another kind of encryption.”

Encryption is reversible by design — the right key recovers the message. Hashing is irreversible by design — nothing recovers the input, ever, for anyone. They solve different problems: encryption protects secrecy; hashing proves integrity. Confusing them leads to real bugs, like “encrypting” passwords when they should be hashed.

Myth “If a public key encrypted it, the public key can decrypt it.”

No — the pair is a one-way street in each direction. What the public key encrypts, only the private key decrypts (you saw a browser refuse to try in chapter 04). The reverse pairing is what signatures use: what the private key signs, the public key verifies. One key can never undo its own operation.

Myth “The padlock means the website is trustworthy.”

The padlock means the connection is encrypted and you are really talking to the domain in the address bar. A scam site with a convincing name can get a perfectly valid certificate in minutes. HTTPS rules out eavesdroppers and impostors-in-the-middle — it says nothing about the honesty of the site itself.

Myth “Strong encryption makes a system secure.”

Encryption is one layer. Real breaches usually route around it: phished credentials, unpatched servers, malicious insiders, stolen devices with keys in memory. Attackers don’t break AES — they log in. Cryptography protects data in transit and at rest; it cannot protect a careless deployment or a tricked human.

Myth “A bigger key is always meaningfully safer.”

Past a threshold, key size stops being the weak point. AES-128 is already unbrute-forceable with any physical computer; AES-256 adds margin, not a different reality. Attacks target implementations, protocols, and people long before they target the key. (Quantum computing changes this calculus for some algorithms — see the next chapter.)

Myth “Inventing my own cipher makes me safer — nobody knows how it works.”

The opposite. Every cipher on this page survived decades of public attack by thousands of experts — that scrutiny is the security. Secret home-made designs fail in hours when they finally meet a competent adversary. The field’s first commandment (Kerckhoffs’s principle, 1883): assume the enemy knows the system; only the key is secret.

12 What’s next

The future of cryptography

Cryptography is not finished. A sufficiently large quantum computer would break today’s public-key algorithms — and the replacements are already shipping. Meanwhile, stranger tools are emerging: proofs that reveal nothing, and computation on data that stays encrypted.

The quantum threat

Shor’s algorithm, run on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer, would factor RSA moduli and solve elliptic-curve problems efficiently — breaking RSA, ECC, and classic Diffie–Hellman. No such machine exists yet, but “harvest now, decrypt later” attacks — recording encrypted traffic today to crack in a decade — make the migration urgent now. Symmetric crypto and hashes are far less affected: AES-256 and SHA-256 remain solid.

Post-quantum cryptography

In 2024, NIST standardized the first post-quantum algorithms — ML-KEM (Kyber) for key exchange and ML-DSA (Dilithium) for signatures — built on lattice problems: finding short vectors in high-dimensional grids, hard even for quantum computers. Major browsers and cloud providers already run hybrid classical + post-quantum handshakes on live traffic.

Zero-knowledge proofs

Prove a statement is true while revealing nothing else: that you are over 18 without showing your birthdate, or that a transaction is valid without disclosing amounts. Once a theoretical curiosity, ZK proofs now secure privacy-preserving identity systems and scale blockchains by proving thousands of transactions in one tiny certificate.

Homomorphic encryption

Compute directly on encrypted data: a cloud server adds your encrypted numbers, returning an encrypted sum — without ever being able to read any of them. Still costly, but improving fast, and already used for private database lookups and privacy-preserving analytics. The endgame: services that are useful without ever seeing your data.

13 Capstone

The cryptography lab

Everything on this page, assembled into one secure message pipeline — the same shape as TLS, Signal, or a software update. Send a message from Alice to Bob; every primitive runs for real. Then let Eve tamper with it in transit and watch the defenses trip, layer by layer.

  1. Keys & session setup

    Alice and Bob derive a shared AES-256 session key (as if via Diffie–Hellman); Alice holds an ECDSA signing key pair.

  2. Hash

    The message is fingerprinted with SHA-256.

  3. Sign

    Alice signs the hash with her private key — authenticity and non-repudiation.

  4. Encrypt

    Message + signature are sealed with AES-256-GCM under the session key — confidentiality.

  5. Transit

    The ciphertext crosses the open network, past Eve.

  6. Decrypt & authenticate

    Bob decrypts with the session key; AES-GCM’s built-in authentication tag detects any modification of the ciphertext.

  7. Verify signature

    Bob checks Alice’s signature against her public key — proof the message is really hers and unmodified.
