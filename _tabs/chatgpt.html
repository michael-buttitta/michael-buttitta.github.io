The capstone experience

How ChatGPT Works

Every exhibit on this site — CPUs, memory, GPUs, operating systems, the internet, cryptography, the cloud, AI — working together in the two seconds between pressing Enter and seeing the first word of an answer.

Explain why sunsets are red.

13 chapters · one prompt · ten technologies · live demonstrations

Here is the whole page in one sentence: you type “Explain why sunsets are red.”, press Send, and about two seconds later words start appearing — and in between, your keystroke crossed an operating system, your prompt was encrypted and routed across the internet backbone, a load balancer picked a server in a data center, your sentence was chopped into tokens and turned into vectors, thousands of GPU cores multiplied enormous matrices dozens of times, a probability distribution was sampled one token at a time, and each token raced back across the planet to be drawn on your screen. Every chapter below follows that same single prompt one stage further. None of this describes any one company’s exact system — it is the common architecture that most modern AI assistants share.

01 Your computer

ChatGPT does not begin in the cloud

It begins under your fingertip. Before your prompt touches any network, it lives entirely inside your own machine: a keyboard interrupt, an operating-system event, a browser buffer in RAM, and a network stack assembling an HTTP request. The first few milliseconds of every ChatGPT conversation are pure CPU, memory, and operating system.

  1. Keyboard scancode + hardware interrupt
  2. Operating system interrupt handler → focused window
  3. Browser event loop → input field
  4. Memory UTF-8 bytes in the browser’s RAM
  5. Network stack HTTPS request → socket → packets

Each keystroke repeats the first three stages; Send triggers the last two. The full mechanics live in the CPU, Memory, and Operating Systems exhibits.

02 The internet

Your prompt crosses the planet

The browser first asks DNS to turn the chat service’s domain name into an IP address, then sends your encrypted prompt hop by hop: your router, your ISP, the internet backbone, and finally a cloud region that might be hundreds or thousands of kilometers away. This is the exact journey the Internet exhibit follows in fifteen chapters — compressed here into one strip.

03 Secure communication

Nobody on the path can read your prompt

Every router, ISP, and backbone operator between you and the data center handles your packets — and none of them can read your prompt. Before a single word travels, your browser and the server perform a TLS handshake: the server proves its identity with a certificate, both sides agree on a shared secret using public-key cryptography, and everything after that is ciphertext. The mathematics behind each step is the subject of the Cryptography exhibit.

  1. ClientHello “here are the ciphers I speak”
  2. Certificate signed identity from the server
  3. Verification signature chain checked locally
  4. Key exchange shared secret, never transmitted
  5. Encrypted session prompt sent as ciphertext
on the wire: Explain why sunsets are red.

Certificates, Diffie–Hellman, and symmetric ciphers each get a full interactive chapter in Understanding Cryptography.

04 The cloud

One prompt among millions

Your prompt arrives at a cloud region where it is one request among a constant torrent. A load balancer spreads incoming requests across many application servers; the application server checks your session and forwards the prompt to an inference service; the inference service queues it for a GPU cluster. No single machine could serve everyone — the whole point of the cloud is that capacity is a fleet, not a box.

Inference is distributed because demand is bursty and models are huge: a large model may not even fit on one GPU, so a single reply can involve several GPUs working in concert. Regions, availability zones, and auto-scaling are covered in Understanding Cloud Computing.

05 Tokenization

The model never sees your words

A language model doesn’t read letters or words. The first thing that happens inside the inference server is that your prompt is chopped into tokens — frequent chunks of text that might be a whole word (sunset), a piece of one (sun + sets), or punctuation. Each token maps to a number, and each number to a vector. Tokens are a compromise: characters would make sequences enormously long, whole words couldn’t handle names or typos, and subwords get both — a fixed vocabulary that can still spell anything.

06 Embeddings

Meaning becomes geometry

Each token ID looks up a vector with thousands of dimensions — its embedding. These vectors are learned during training so that tokens used in similar contexts end up near each other. Meaning stops being a dictionary definition and becomes a location: “cat” is close to “dog”, far from “pizza”, and the direction between related words often carries meaning too.

Hover or tap a word to see its nearest neighbors.

Real embeddings have thousands of dimensions; this map is a hand-made 2-D illustration of the one property that matters: similar meaning → nearby points. Distances here are for intuition, not measurements from a real model. For distances that are measurements, Large Language Models builds its space from real co-occurrence counts, weights them by pointwise mutual information, and projects the result live.

07 The transformer

A stack of layers, one job: predict the next token

The embeddings now flow through the transformer — dozens of near-identical layers stacked on top of each other. Each layer does two things: attention lets every token gather information from the tokens before it, and a small feed-forward network transforms what was gathered. Layer by layer, the vectors stop representing isolated tokens and start representing your sentence’s meaning. At the top, the model emits one thing only: a probability for every token in its vocabulary to come next.

Embedding layer
Prediction head — scores for every token in the vocabulary

Deliberately conceptual: real models have many more layers, multiple attention heads per layer, normalization, and residual connections. The intuition — embed, repeatedly mix context and transform, then score the vocabulary — is the honest core. For how the model learned its weights in the first place, see Understanding AI on training vs inference.

08 Attention

Every word looks back at the words before it

Attention is the transformer’s superpower. As the model processes each token, it computes how relevant every earlier token is, and blends their information in proportion. That is how “it” can mean the animal in one sentence and the street in a nearly identical one — the model doesn’t follow a grammar rule, it learned where to look. Hover any token below.

The first two sentences are a classic pair: only the last word changes, yet “it” shifts its strongest attention from animal to street. Weights here are hand-crafted to illustrate the behavior; a real model computes them fresh in every one of dozens of layers, with many attention heads looking for different patterns at once. To watch that computation instead of an illustration of it, Large Language Models runs scaled dot-product attention on real query, key and value matrices — small enough that every number fits on screen.

09 GPU inference

Why this needs thousands of cores

Everything the transformer does — attention, feed-forward layers, the final vocabulary scores — is matrix multiplication: millions of independent multiply-and-add operations with no need to wait for each other. That is precisely the workload a GPU was built for. A CPU races through a handful of operations at a time; a GPU does tens of thousands simultaneously — and keeping its cores fed is why memory bandwidth matters as much as raw compute.

Illustrative speeds, honest shape: both sides compute the same matrix, the GPU just does it in wide parallel waves. Inside a real inference step this happens hundreds of times per token — which is why models are served from GPU clusters, not laptops. The full story is in Inside a Modern GPU.

10 Choosing the next token

The model proposes; the sampler decides

The transformer’s output is not a word — it is a probability for every token in the vocabulary. Something still has to pick one. Always taking the most likely token gives safe, repetitive text; sampling from the full distribution occasionally picks nonsense. Real systems steer between the two with three dials: temperature reshapes the distribution, top-k keeps only the k likeliest tokens, and top-p keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p. Drag them and watch the same model produce different prose.

11 Streaming

Why the answer appears word by word

The model produces one token at a time — each new token requires a full pass through every transformer layer, with all the text so far as context. Rather than make you stare at a spinner until the last token is done, the server streams each token back over the open HTTPS connection the moment it exists. Your OS delivers the bytes to the browser, the browser appends them to the page, and your CPU re-renders — dozens of times per answer.

13 Costs & scale

What it takes to answer everyone at once

Multiply this page by everyone using AI assistants at the same moment: millions of concurrent conversations, each one running the full transformer once per token. Serving that means fleets of GPU servers across cloud regions, petabytes of fast storage holding model weights and logs, and networks moving tokens between machines with microsecond budgets. Every operator works the same trade-off triangle: latency (how fast your tokens arrive), throughput (how many users share each GPU), and energy (what the whole thing costs to run).

“I understand what happens every time I send a prompt.”

A keystroke became an interrupt, an interrupt became a request, a request became ciphertext, ciphertext crossed the backbone, a load balancer chose a server, a tokenizer chopped your sentence, thousands of GPU cores pushed vectors through a transformer, a sampler rolled weighted dice one token at a time, and the answer streamed back to be painted on your screen — all in about the time it takes to blink twice. And one distinction is worth keeping: the model predicts; it does not understand the way you do. It is statistical pattern completion, executed on an extraordinary stack of engineering — which makes it more impressive to understand, not less.
